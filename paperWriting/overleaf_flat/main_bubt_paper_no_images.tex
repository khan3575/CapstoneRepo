% ---- Abstract ----
\begin{center}
    \section*{Abstract}
\end{center}

Brain tumor segmentation in multi-modal MRI remains computationally prohibitive for
real-time clinical deployment when approached through volumetric deep learning
pipelines. This paper presents a superpixel-graph formulation that reframes 3D MRI
segmentation as a node-classification problem on 2D spatial graphs, enabling
inference with a fraction of the memory and latency of comparable volumetric methods.
Each brain MRI volume is decomposed into a sequence of 2-slice graphs using SLIC
superpixels (200 per slice), with 15-dimensional node features encoding multi-modal
intensity statistics, spatial coordinates, and morphological descriptors. A 5-layer
GraphSAGE network with 256-dimensional hidden layers (439,041 parameters total) is
trained end-to-end using BCEWithLogitsLoss on the BraTS 2021 dataset under a
5-fold patient-level cross-validation protocol. The model achieves a mean
cross-validation Dice of \textbf{90.02\%\,$\pm$\,0.66\%} across 1,000 patients and
an ensemble Dice of \textbf{91.41\%} on a sealed 251-patient held-out test set.
Zero-shot transfer to BraTS 2023 yields \textbf{89.40\% Dice} across 1,245 unseen
patients, a generalisation gap of 2.01 percentage points. End-to-end inference per
patient requires 1,732\,ms including on-the-fly graph construction; for pre-built
graphs, GNN inference alone runs in \textbf{75.4\,ms}, peaking at \textbf{11\,MB}
GPU memory --- 227 times less than a comparable 3D U-Net. These results suggest that
graph-structured representations provide a memory-efficient alternative to volumetric
convolution for binary tumor detection, with cross-dataset generalisation competitive
with methods that require full retraining.

GraphSAGE, BraTS 2021, BraTS 2023, Computational Efficiency, Superpixel Graph,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Brain tumour segmentation determines the margin between adequate and inadequate
oncological care. Before a neurosurgeon can plan a resection, before a radiation
oncologist can delineate a target volume, and before a neuro-oncologist can assess
treatment response, the tumour boundary must be traced with sub-millimetre precision
across multiple MRI modalities. Manual delineation by a trained radiologist requires
90 to 120 minutes per case \cite{menze2015multimodal}; for a centre processing 40
cases per week, this represents 60 to 80 radiologist-hours devoted entirely to
contouring. Automated segmentation has therefore been a sustained research priority
for two decades.

The Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) challenge \cite{menze2015multimodal, bakas2021brats}
has anchored this priority to a reproducible benchmark. Annually updated datasets of
expert-annotated glioma cases---covering T1-weighted (T1), contrast-enhanced T1
(T1CE), T2-weighted (T2), and Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR)
modalities---have driven successive generations of model development. The leading
current methods are volumetric convolutional networks: nnU-Net
\cite{isensee2021nnunet} achieves $\sim$92.7\% Whole Tumour (WT) Dice on BraTS 2021
at 31 million parameters; Swin-UNETR \cite{hatamizadeh2022swin} reaches 93.3\% at 62
million parameters. These numbers are clinically meaningful.

The obstacle is the infrastructure they require. nnU-Net demands $>$2.5\,GB GPU
inference memory; Swin-UNETR requires comparable or greater resources, with
per-patient inference taking 10 to 30 seconds on high-end hardware. For the majority
of hospitals globally where advanced GPU hardware is absent, these requirements make
state-of-the-art brain tumour AI effectively unavailable. The accuracy of nnU-Net
is irrelevant to a radiologist whose workstation cannot run it.

We identified a structural mismatch at the root of this problem. Brain tumours occupy
on average 5--10\% of total brain volume. Volumetric CNNs nonetheless process every
voxel in the complete 3D grid---dense computation on a spatially sparse signal. The
computational cost scales with the full volume, not with the tumour. Graph Neural
Networks \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} address this mismatch by design: rather than
voxels, a GNN operates on superpixel nodes---spatially compact clusters of adjacent
voxels produced by SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic}. This collapses the spatial
representation by orders of magnitude while preserving the multi-modal intensity
patterns that discriminate tumour from healthy tissue.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Accurate brain tumour segmentation from MRI is required before any treatment
decision can proceed --- surgical planning, radiation targeting, and response
monitoring all depend on knowing where the tumour boundary lies. A trained
radiologist needs 90 to 120 minutes to trace this boundary manually across
multiple MRI sequences \cite{menze2015multimodal}. At a centre handling 40 cases
per week, that is 60 to 80 hours of radiologist time spent on contouring alone.
Automating this task frees that time for diagnosis and patient care.

The best automated methods --- nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet}, Swin-UNETR
\cite{hatamizadeh2022swin} --- use 3D convolutions that process the entire MRI
volume as a dense grid. Every voxel in a $240 \times 240 \times 155$ volume
receives the same computation, whether it belongs to the tumour or to healthy
tissue. Gliomas, however, typically make up only 5--10\% of the brain volume.
The remaining 90\% or more is healthy tissue that the model must process anyway,
because convolution treats every voxel the same. The computational cost therefore
scales with the full volume, not with the tumour. In practice, this means models with 31--62 million parameters
\cite{isensee2021nnunet, hatamizadeh2022swin} that require multiple gigabytes
of GPU memory and per-patient processing times measured in seconds to minutes
on high-end hardware. Most hospitals do not have that hardware.

Graph neural networks offer a way around this. Instead of processing every voxel,
a GNN operates on superpixels --- groups of similar neighbouring pixels produced
by algorithms like SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic}. A typical BraTS slice reduces
from $240 \times 240 = 57{,}600$ voxels to roughly 46 superpixel nodes. The graph
captures which regions are next to each other, and each node carries a feature
vector summarising the intensity and spatial properties of its region. This
representation is far smaller than the original volume, but it keeps the
information the model needs to tell tumour from non-tumour tissue apart. Yet
existing GNN work in brain tumour segmentation has not been tested at scale:
studies use small patient sets, operate on individual 2D slices without
cross-slice connectivity, and do not report controlled efficiency comparisons
against CNN baselines on the same hardware \cite{kipf2017gcn, hamilton2017graphsage}.
No published work evaluates a GNN ensemble on the full BraTS 2021 dataset under a
sealed held-out protocol with end-to-end timing benchmarks.

This work asks a direct question: can a superpixel-based graph neural network
segment brain tumours from multi-modal 3D MRI with accuracy competitive to
volumetric CNNs, while using a fraction of the parameters, memory, and inference
time? Answering this requires training a competitive model, then validating it
through patient-level cross-validation, sealed held-out evaluation, and controlled
benchmarking --- none of which has been done for graph-based brain tumour
segmentation before.

\subsection{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

This research targets four measurable objectives, each verifiable against
the experimental results in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Graph construction pipeline.} Build a preprocessing
    pipeline that converts each 4-modality BraTS 2021 MRI volume into a
    set of superpixel graphs using SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic}, with
    15-dimensional node features encoding intensity statistics, spatial
    position, and region shape. The pipeline must produce zero
    ground-truth label leakage, confirmed by 15 automated integrity
    checks.

    \item \textbf{Competitive GNN segmentation.} Train a GraphSAGE-based
    \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} binary node classifier with fewer than
    500\,K trainable parameters to achieve mean Dice $\geq 90\%$ on a
    sealed 251-patient held-out test set under 5-fold patient-level
    cross-validation across 1,000 training patients. A soft-voting
    ensemble of the five fold-best models should target Dice above 91\%,
    within reach of leading volumetric CNNs on the same benchmark.

    \item \textbf{Efficiency benchmarking.} Measure end-to-end
    per-patient inference time, peak GPU memory, parameter count, and
    model size on disk for both the proposed GNN and a 3D U-Net baseline
    \cite{cicek20163d} trained on the same data, using identical
    hardware. The GNN must demonstrate at least a $5\times$ speedup over
    the baseline in end-to-end inference.

    \item \textbf{Cross-edition generalisation.} Evaluate the trained
    ensemble on all 1,245 BraTS 2023 patients without any retraining or
    threshold adjustment, and measure the Dice gap relative to BraTS 2021
    held-out performance. A gap under 5 percentage points confirms that
    the model generalises across dataset editions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

GNN-based brain tumour segmentation has not been evaluated at the scale
or rigour that the BraTS benchmark demands. Three specific gaps separate
existing GNN work from a credible comparison with volumetric CNNs.

\textbf{No ensemble evaluation.} Soft-voting ensembles are standard
practice in volumetric CNN pipelines. nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet}
trains five folds and averages their predictions; the resulting ensemble
typically improves Dice by 1--3 percentage points over any single fold.
BraTS leaderboard rankings reflect ensemble results, not single-model
results, so any method claiming competitive accuracy must be evaluated
the same way. No published work trains a multi-fold GNN on BraTS and
reports ensemble Dice on a sealed held-out set. Without this, comparing
a single-fold GNN to an ensemble CNN is not a fair comparison.

\textbf{No controlled efficiency measurement.} The practical argument
for graph-based segmentation is efficiency: fewer parameters, less
memory, faster inference. But this argument requires measurement, not
assumption. Saueressig et al.\ \cite{saueressig2021exploring} apply
GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT to BraTS~2019 supervoxel graphs and claim
``5 to 15 times faster'' training than CNNs, yet report no inference
time, no GPU memory usage, and no parameter count. Patel et
al.\ \cite{patel2023multiclass} train a GAT on BraTS~2021 with 10.2M
parameters and measure 1.7\,s average inference --- but on a Tesla K80,
with no CNN baseline tested on the same device. Neither study runs a
controlled comparison where both a GNN and a CNN are benchmarked on
identical hardware under identical conditions. Without such a
comparison, the efficiency advantage of GNNs over CNNs for brain tumour
segmentation remains a hypothesis, not a result.

\textbf{No cross-edition generalisation test.} BraTS releases a new
dataset edition each year with different patients, institutions, and
sometimes different annotation protocols \cite{menze2015multimodal,
bakas2021brats}. Standard CNN pipelines retrain per edition
\cite{isensee2021nnunet}. Whether a superpixel-based GNN trained on one
edition can segment patients from a different edition --- without
retraining or threshold adjustment --- has not been tested. This is a
direct test of whether the graph representation captures features that
transfer across acquisition differences, or whether it overfits to the
training data.

This work addresses all three gaps: a 5-fold soft-voting ensemble
evaluated on a sealed 251-patient held-out set; controlled benchmarking
of inference time, memory, parameters, and model size against a 3D U-Net
\cite{cicek20163d} baseline on identical hardware; and zero-shot
evaluation on 1,245 BraTS 2023 patients with no retraining.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This paper presents a complete system built on this principle. We make five
contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Superpixel-graph pipeline.} A preprocessing and graph construction
    pipeline that converts 3D BraTS volumes into 2-slice SLIC graphs with
    15-dimensional multi-modal node features encoding intensity statistics, spatial
    coordinates, and morphological properties. Ground-truth labels are excluded from
    all node features; strict leakage isolation was verified by audit and confirmed by
    integrity check 14 of our 15-point protocol.

    \item \textbf{GraphSAGE binary classifier.} A 5-layer GraphSAGE network
    (439,041 parameters) trained end-to-end with BCEWithLogitsLoss, AdamW
    \cite{loshchilov2019adamw}, OneCycleLR scheduling, and automatic mixed precision
    under a 5-fold patient-level cross-validation protocol with 15 automated
    data-integrity checks. Mean cross-validation Dice:
    \textbf{90.02\%\,$\pm$\,0.66\%} across 1,000 patients.

    \item \textbf{5-fold soft-voting ensemble.} Soft-voting aggregation of five
    fold-best checkpoints achieves \textbf{91.41\% Dice} on a sealed 251-patient
    held-out set---a $+1.39$\,pp lift over single-fold inference and the primary
    evaluation result of this paper.

    \item \textbf{Zero-shot cross-edition evaluation.} Direct evaluation on BraTS
    2023 (1,245 patients, no retraining) yields \textbf{89.40\% Dice}, a 2.01\,pp
    generalisation gap, confirming cross-edition robustness without domain adaptation.

    \item \textbf{Efficiency benchmarking.} GNN inference on pre-built graphs:
    \textbf{75.4\,ms} and \textbf{11\,MB} peak GPU memory per patient. End-to-end
    including on-the-fly graph construction: \textbf{1,732\,ms}---a
    \textbf{5.9$\times$ speedup} over a hardware-matched 3D U-Net, with
    \textbf{155$\times$ fewer parameters} and \textbf{227$\times$ lower peak GPU
    memory}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Volumetric CNN Methods for Brain Tumour Segmentation}

The original 2D U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} established skip-connection
encoder-decoder segmentation as the dominant paradigm in medical imaging. Its 3D
extension \cite{cicek20163d} applies volumetric convolutions with skip connections
to produce dense voxel-level predictions from 3D MRI volumes. Attention U-Net
\cite{oktay2018attention} added soft attention gates to focus computation on
relevant anatomical regions. nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet} automated the entire
pipeline---preprocessing normalisation, patch size, training schedule---from dataset
fingerprints, achieving $\sim$92.7\% Whole Tumour Dice on BraTS 2021 at 31 million
parameters. TransBTS \cite{wang2021transbts} integrated multi-scale Transformer
encoders into a U-Net backbone for global context capture (90.1\% WT Dice, 33M
parameters). Swin-UNETR \cite{hatamizadeh2022swin} replaced CNN encoders with
hierarchical Swin Transformers \cite{liu2021swin}, reaching 93.3\% WT Dice at 62
million parameters.

A defining characteristic of all these methods is that inference memory and latency
scale with the 3D volume resolution. Reported GPU memory requirements range from
2.5\,GB (nnU-Net) to over 6\,GB (Swin-UNETR); per-patient inference times on
high-end hardware are 10 to 30 seconds. On the RTX 2060 (6\,GB VRAM) used in this
work, a binary 3D U-Net consumes the full memory budget at $\sim$10.2 seconds per
patient. The computational bottleneck is therefore not accuracy---these methods are
clinically accurate---but the hardware barrier to deployment.

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks in Medical Imaging}

Spectral graph convolutions \cite{kipf2017gcn} demonstrated competitive
node-classification performance on graph-structured data. GraphSAGE
\cite{hamilton2017graphsage} extended graph learning to inductive settings via
neighbourhood aggregation (mean, max, or LSTM sampling), enabling generalisation
to nodes absent from training. Graph Attention Networks (GAT)
\cite{velickovic2018gat} introduced attention-weighted message passing to
adaptively weight neighbour contributions. V-Net \cite{milletari2016vnet}
demonstrated that fully convolutional volumetric segmentation could be trained
with Dice loss directly, influencing graph-based objective formulations. Point cloud
methods \cite{qi2017pointnet} introduced learnable aggregation over irregular
3D structures, establishing the broader family of non-grid deep learning.

GNN approaches have been applied to medical imaging for anatomical shape modelling
on organ meshes, disease classification via population graphs, and histopathology
patch graph analysis. In the brain tumour domain, graph representations derived
from superpixels and region adjacency have been explored for 2D slice-level
classification, but application to 3D multi-modal MRI segmentation with rigorous
patient-level cross-validation, data-integrity auditing, and efficiency benchmarking
against volumetric baselines remains unaddressed. No published work with a directly
comparable binary whole-tumour BraTS 2021 experimental protocol was identified at
the time of submission. This work is, to our knowledge, the first systematic
efficiency analysis of GNN-based binary brain tumour segmentation on BraTS.

\subsection{Superpixel-Based Representations}

SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic} clusters spatially adjacent pixels into compact regions
through iterative $k$-means in a 5-dimensional feature space (colour channels plus
normalised spatial coordinates). Its $O(n)$ runtime, parameter simplicity (target
segment count, compactness), and spatial regularity make it the most widely used
superpixel algorithm in medical imaging pipelines. Superpixel-based representations
have been applied to retinal vessel segmentation, histopathology patch analysis, and
2D cardiac MRI, where they reduce the dense pixel graph to a compact semantically
coherent node set.

Adapting SLIC to 3D multi-modal MRI introduces non-trivial challenges absent from
2D applications: inter-slice connectivity must be explicitly modelled; normalisation
must account for modality-specific intensity ranges; and feature engineering must
fuse information from four co-registered channels without leaking ground-truth
labels. Our pipeline addresses all three challenges, validated by a 15-point
data-integrity protocol run prior to each training fold.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Proposed Model and Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

The proposed system is a four-stage pipeline that maps a 3D multi-modal MRI volume
to a binary whole-tumour mask via an intermediate graph representation. Formally,
the pipeline implements the transformation:
\[
    \hat{M} = \Phi\!\left(\mathcal{G}(V)\right),
    \quad V \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 240 \times 240 \times 155},
    \quad \hat{M} \in \{0,1\}^{240 \times 240 \times 155}
\]
where $V$ is the four-modality (T1, T1CE, T2, FLAIR) MRI volume, $\mathcal{G}(\cdot)$
is the graph construction operator, and $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the trained GraphSAGE
classifier. The four stages are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Volumetric preprocessing.} Per-modality Z-score normalisation
    within the skull-stripped brain mask; active axial slice selection by brain-fraction
    threshold; grouping into 2-slice graph units.

    \item \textbf{Superpixel graph construction.} SLIC superpixel segmentation on
    each 2-slice unit; 15-dimensional node feature extraction from all four MRI
    modalities; intra-slice and inter-slice edge construction via Region Adjacency
    Graph (RAG) and $k$-nearest-neighbour matching.

    \item \textbf{GraphSAGE node classification.} A 5-layer GraphSAGE network with
    256-dimensional hidden representations performs binary node classification
    (tumour / non-tumour) on each graph independently.

    \item \textbf{Ensemble inference.} Five fold-best checkpoints are aggregated by
    soft-voting to produce the final per-node prediction; predicted tumour nodes are
    back-projected to voxel space to reconstruct the binary mask $\hat{M}$.
\end{enumerate}

The complete pipeline achieves a spatial compression ratio of ${\approx}2{,}284\times$
relative to full voxel resolution (Section~\ref{sec:graphconst}), enabling
end-to-end inference in 1,732\,ms and GNN-only inference in 75.4\,ms on an
NVIDIA RTX 2060 with 11\,MB peak GPU memory.

\subsection{Mathematical Description of the Pipeline}
\label{sec:mathpipeline}

\textbf{Step 1 --- Per-modality Z-score normalisation.}
Let $V_m \in \mathbb{R}^{240 \times 240 \times 155}$ denote the volume for modality
$m \in \{\text{T1}, \text{T1CE}, \text{T2}, \text{FLAIR}\}$ and let
$\mathcal{B} \subset \{1,\dots,240\}^2 \times \{1,\dots,155\}$ be the set of voxel
indices within the skull-stripped brain mask. The normalised volume is:
\[
    \tilde{V}_m(i) = \frac{V_m(i) - \mu_m}{\sigma_m},
    \quad i \in \mathcal{B}
\]
where $\mu_m = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} V_m(i)$ and
$\sigma_m = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}}(V_m(i)-\mu_m)^2}$
are the intra-patient, within-mask mean and standard deviation for modality $m$.
No inter-patient normalisation is applied.

\textbf{Step 2 --- Active slice selection.}
A slice $z \in \{1,\dots,155\}$ is classified as active if the fraction of
non-zero brain-mask voxels in that slice exceeds a threshold $\tau = 0.05$:
\[
    z \text{ is active} \iff
    \frac{|\{(x,y) : (x,y,z) \in \mathcal{B}\}|}{240 \times 240} \geq \tau
\]
Consecutive active slices are grouped into non-overlapping 2-slice units
$\mathcal{S}_j = \{z_{2j-1},\, z_{2j}\}$. The empirically measured mean is
$137.5 \pm 6.9$ active slices per patient, yielding $\approx$68--69 graph
units per patient.

\textbf{Step 3 --- SLIC superpixel segmentation.}
For each 2-slice unit $\mathcal{S}_j$, SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic} is applied to the
T1CE channel. SLIC minimises a weighted distance in the 3-dimensional feature space
$(I, x, y)$, where $I$ is normalised intensity and $(x,y)$ are spatial coordinates.
The per-cluster assignment minimises:
\[
    D_s(p,\,c_k) = \sqrt{d_I^2 + \left(\frac{d_{xy}}{S}\right)^2 m^2}
\]
where $d_I = |I_p - I_{c_k}|$ is the intensity distance, $d_{xy}$ is the
Euclidean spatial distance, $S = \sqrt{N / K}$ is the grid interval for $K$
target segments in an $N$-pixel image, and $m = 0.1$ is the compactness parameter
controlling the regularity--boundary adherence trade-off. The algorithm
runs until cluster assignments converge or a maximum iteration count is reached.
Parameters used: \texttt{n\_segments}=200, \texttt{compactness}=0.1,
\texttt{sigma}=0.3. The mean realised segment count is $\approx$46 per slice.

\textbf{Step 4 --- Node feature extraction.}
Each superpixel $s_i$ is represented by a 15-dimensional feature vector
$\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{15}$:
\[
    \mathbf{x}_i =
    \bigl[\,
        \underbrace{\mu_{i,m},\; \sigma_{i,m}}_{\text{intensity stats},\; m \in \{
        \text{T1},\text{T1CE},\text{T2},\text{FLAIR}\}},\;
        \underbrace{|s_i|,\; \tilde{a}_i,\; \tilde{y}_i,\; \tilde{x}_i}_{\text{spatial}},\;
        \underbrace{P_i,\; C_i,\; R_i}_{\text{morphological}}
    \,\bigr]^\top
\]
where $\mu_{i,m}$ and $\sigma_{i,m}$ are the per-modality within-superpixel mean
and standard deviation (8 features); $|s_i|$ is superpixel area, $\tilde{a}_i$
is area normalised by slice area, $\tilde{y}_i$ and $\tilde{x}_i$ are centroid
coordinates normalised to $[0,1]$ (4 features); and $P_i$, $C_i = 4\pi|s_i|/P_i^2$,
$R_i = \max_{m}(\mu_{i,m}) - \min_m(\mu_{i,m})$ are perimeter, compactness, and
intensity range respectively (3 features). No ground-truth label is included in
$\mathbf{x}_i$.

\subsection{Mathematical Description of Segmentation}
\label{sec:mathseg}

\textbf{Node label assignment.}
The binary node label $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ is assigned based on voxel-level overlap
between superpixel $s_i$ and the Whole Tumour ground-truth mask $M_{\text{GT}}$:
\[
    y_i = \mathbf{1}\!\left[
        \frac{|s_i \cap M_{\text{GT}}|}{|s_i|} > 0.5
    \right]
\]
A majority-vote threshold of 50\% is applied: a node is positive if more than half
of its constituent voxels lie within the Whole Tumour annotation.

\textbf{GraphSAGE message passing.}
The GraphSAGE operator \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} at layer $l$ computes node
embedding $\mathbf{h}_v^{(l)}$ by aggregating mean-pooled neighbourhood representations
concatenated with the node's own embedding:
\[
    \mathbf{h}_v^{(l)} = \sigma\!\left(
        \mathbf{W}^{(l)} \cdot \mathrm{CONCAT}\!\left(
            \mathbf{h}_v^{(l-1)},\;
            \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(v)|}
            \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \mathbf{h}_u^{(l-1)}
        \right)
    \right)
\]
where $\mathbf{W}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l \times 2d_{l-1}}$ is the learnable
weight matrix at layer $l$, $\mathcal{N}(v)$ is the one-hop neighbour set of node
$v$, and $\sigma$ is the ReLU non-linearity. Each layer applies BatchNorm before
ReLU and Dropout (rate 0.1) after. The input dimension is $d_0 = 15$; all
five hidden layers use $d_l = 256$.

\textbf{Binary classification and loss.}
The final layer maps the node embedding to a scalar logit
$z_v = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{h}_v^{(5)} + b$, $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$.
Training minimises BCEWithLogitsLoss with positive class weight $w^+ = 9.0$:
\[
    \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \left[
        w^+ \cdot y_v \log \sigma(z_v) + (1 - y_v) \log(1 - \sigma(z_v))
    \right]
\]
where $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$ is the sigmoid function. The weight $w^+ = 9.0$
compensates for a $\approx$19:1 background-to-tumour class imbalance at the
superpixel level. At inference, the decision threshold is $\tau_d = 0.5$:
$\hat{y}_v = \mathbf{1}[\sigma(z_v) \geq 0.5]$.

\textbf{Dice coefficient.}
The evaluation metric is the Sørensen-Dice coefficient computed at patient level
by aggregating node predictions to voxel masks:
\[
    \mathrm{Dice}(M_{\text{pred}},\, M_{\text{GT}}) =
    \frac{2\,|M_{\text{pred}} \cap M_{\text{GT}}|}
         {|M_{\text{pred}}| + |M_{\text{GT}}|}
\]

\subsection{Proposed Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} summarises the complete inference procedure for a
single patient volume.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Superpixel-GNN Inference Pipeline}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\KwIn{MRI volume $V \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 240 \times 240 \times 155}$,
      brain mask $\mathcal{B}$,
      trained ensemble $\{\Phi_k\}_{k=1}^{5}$}
\KwOut{Binary whole-tumour mask $\hat{M} \in \{0,1\}^{240 \times 240 \times 155}$}
\BlankLine
\tcp{Stage 1: Preprocessing}
\For{each modality $m \in \{\mathrm{T1}, \mathrm{T1CE}, \mathrm{T2}, \mathrm{FLAIR}\}$}{
    Compute $\mu_m$, $\sigma_m$ over $\mathcal{B}$\;
    $\tilde{V}_m \leftarrow (V_m - \mu_m) / \sigma_m$ on $\mathcal{B}$\;
}
$\mathcal{A} \leftarrow$ active slices with brain-fraction $\geq 0.05$\;
$\{\mathcal{S}_j\} \leftarrow$ partition $\mathcal{A}$ into consecutive 2-slice units\;
\BlankLine
\tcp{Stage 2: Graph construction}
\ForEach{2-slice unit $\mathcal{S}_j$}{
    $\{s_i\} \leftarrow \mathrm{SLIC}(\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{T1CE}}[\mathcal{S}_j],\;
    K{=}200,\; m{=}0.1)$\;
    \ForEach{superpixel $s_i$}{
        $\mathbf{x}_i \leftarrow$ extract 15-dimensional feature vector\;
    }
    $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{intra}} \leftarrow$ RAG edges from SLIC adjacency\;
    $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{inter}} \leftarrow$ $k$NN edges across slices ($k{=}3$,
    IoU $> 0.1$, dist $< 10\,\mathrm{mm}$)\;
    $\mathcal{G}_j \leftarrow (\{s_i\},\; \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{intra}} \cup
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{inter}},\; \{\mathbf{x}_i\})$\;
}
\BlankLine
\tcp{Stage 3: Ensemble node classification}
\ForEach{graph $\mathcal{G}_j$}{
    \For{$k = 1$ \KwTo $5$}{
        $\mathbf{z}^{(k)} \leftarrow \Phi_k(\mathcal{G}_j)$ \tcp*{scalar logits per node}
    }
    $P_{\mathrm{ens}}(v) \leftarrow \frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{5} \sigma(z_v^{(k)})$
    \ForEach{node $v$}{
        $\hat{y}_v \leftarrow \mathbf{1}[P_{\mathrm{ens}}(v) \geq 0.5]$\;
    }
}
\BlankLine
\tcp{Stage 4: Voxel mask reconstruction}
$\hat{M} \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$\;
\ForEach{superpixel $s_i$ with $\hat{y}_{s_i} = 1$}{
    $\hat{M}[\text{voxels}(s_i)] \leftarrow 1$\;
}
\Return{$\hat{M}$}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Graph Construction}
\label{sec:graphconst}

\textbf{Formal graph definition.}
Each 2-slice MRI unit $\mathcal{S}_j$ is represented as an undirected attributed
graph $\mathcal{G}_j = (\mathcal{V}_j,\, \mathcal{E}_j,\, \mathbf{X}_j)$, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{V}_j = \{v_1, \dots, v_{n_j}\}$ is the node set; each node
    corresponds to one SLIC superpixel, $n_j \approx 92$ (2 slices
    $\times$ $\approx$46 superpixels per slice on average);
    \item $\mathcal{E}_j \subseteq \mathcal{V}_j \times \mathcal{V}_j$ is the
    edge set, partitioned as
    $\mathcal{E}_j = \mathcal{E}_j^{\text{intra}} \cup \mathcal{E}_j^{\text{inter}}$
    (intra-slice adjacency and inter-slice proximity edges);
    \item $\mathbf{X}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{n_j \times 15}$ is the node feature matrix,
    with row $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{15}$ as defined in
    Section~\ref{sec:mathpipeline}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Intra-slice edge set.}
Two nodes $v_i$ and $v_j$ within the same slice $z$ are connected by an intra-slice
edge if their corresponding superpixels are spatially adjacent in the SLIC
segmentation map, i.e., they share at least one pixel boundary:
\[
    (v_i, v_j) \in \mathcal{E}^{\text{intra}} \iff
    \exists\, p \in s_i,\, q \in s_j :
    \|p - q\|_1 = 1, \quad z(p) = z(q)
\]
This is equivalent to extracting the Region Adjacency Graph (RAG) from the SLIC
label map using 4-connectivity. For a slice with $K \approx 46$ superpixels,
the resulting RAG has $O(K)$ edges.

\textbf{Inter-slice edge set.}
Two nodes $v_i$ (in slice $z$) and $v_j$ (in slice $z+1$) are connected by an
inter-slice edge if their superpixels satisfy all three proximity criteria
simultaneously:
\[
    (v_i, v_j) \in \mathcal{E}^{\text{inter}} \iff
    \mathrm{IoU}(s_i, s_j) > 0.1 \;\wedge\;
    \|\mathbf{c}_i - \mathbf{c}_j\|_2 < 10\,\text{mm} \;\wedge\;
    j \in \mathrm{kNN}_k(i),\; k = 3
\]
where $\mathrm{IoU}(s_i, s_j) = |s_i \cap s_j| / |s_i \cup s_j|$ is the
Intersection-over-Union of the projected pixel sets, $\mathbf{c}_i$ and
$\mathbf{c}_j$ are the centroid coordinates in mm, and $\mathrm{kNN}_k(i)$
denotes the $k$-nearest neighbours of $v_i$ by centroid Euclidean distance.
These three conditions jointly ensure inter-slice connections are spatially
coherent and topologically meaningful.

\textbf{Example.} For a representative patient with 137 active slices, the graph
construction produces $\approx$68 graph units. Each unit contains on average
$n_j \approx 92$ nodes and $|\mathcal{E}_j| \approx 180$ edges (130 intra-slice
+ 50 inter-slice), yielding a mean node degree of $\approx$3.9. The full patient
graph representation contains $\approx$3,909 nodes compared to 8,928,000 raw
voxels --- a compression ratio of $\approx$2,284$\times$.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Each MRI volume undergoes Z-score normalisation per
modality within the skull-stripped brain mask (zero-mean, unit-variance). No
inter-patient normalisation is applied.

\textbf{Adaptive slice selection.} Of 155 axial slices per patient, active slices
are identified by a minimum brain-fraction threshold of 5\%. Consecutive active-slice
pairs are grouped into 2-slice graph units. Mean: $137.5 \pm 6.9$ active slices per
patient, yielding $\approx$68--69 graph units per patient.

\textbf{Superpixel segmentation.} SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic} is applied to the
T1CE channel of each 2-slice unit with parameters: \texttt{n\_segments}=200,
\texttt{compactness}=0.1, \texttt{sigma}=0.3. Mean actual segments: $\approx$46
per slice.

\textbf{Node feature engineering (15-dimensional):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Intensity statistics: per-superpixel mean and standard deviation of T1,
    T1CE, T2, and FLAIR (8 features)
    \item Spatial descriptors: superpixel area, normalised area, normalised
    $y$-centroid, normalised $x$-centroid (4 features)
    \item Morphological features: perimeter, compactness, intensity range (3 features)
\end{itemize}

No ground-truth features are included. rigorous verification confirms the 15-dimensional representation remains free from information leakage.

\textbf{Edge construction.} Intra-slice edges connect SLIC-adjacent superpixels
via the Region Adjacency Graph (RAG). Inter-slice edges link superpixels across
the two slices using $k$-nearest-neighbour matching (IoU threshold $>0.1$,
Euclidean distance $<10$\,mm, $k=3$).

\textbf{Spatial compression.} The measured compression ratio from full voxel-space
to graph-node-space is:
\begin{equation}
    R_{\text{measured}} = \frac{\text{total brain voxels per patient}}
    {\text{mean graph nodes per patient}}
    = \frac{8{,}928{,}000}{3{,}909} \approx 2{,}284\times
    \label{eq:compression}
\end{equation}
where $8{,}928{,}000 = 240 \times 240 \times 155$ total voxels and 3,909 is the
empirically measured mean node count per patient across the 1,000-patient
cross-validation cohort. This ratio reflects the combined effect of background
removal (only brain-fraction voxels are active), slice-level SLIC compression, and
the 2-slice graph grouping strategy.

\textbf{Node labels.} Binary: node label = 1 if $>$50\% of the superpixel's voxels
overlap with the Whole Tumour mask, 0 otherwise.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

A 5-layer GraphSAGE network \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} with mean-pooling
aggregation is used for binary node classification:

\begin{equation}
    h_v^{(l)} = \sigma\!\left(W^{(l)} \cdot \mathrm{CONCAT}\!\left(
    h_v^{(l-1)},\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(v)|}\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}
    h_u^{(l-1)}\right)\right)
\end{equation}

where $h_v^{(l)}$ is the node embedding at layer $l$, $\mathcal{N}(v)$ is the
neighbourhood of node $v$, and $\sigma$ is ReLU. Each of the five layers uses 256
hidden channels followed by BatchNorm, ReLU, and Dropout (0.1). The final linear
layer maps to a scalar logit for binary classification. Implemented with PyTorch
Geometric \cite{fey2019pyg}.

Total trainable parameters: \textbf{439,041}. Full model checkpoint: 5.1\,MB;
compiled weights only: 1.7\,MB on disk.

\subsection{Training Protocol}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loss}: BCEWithLogitsLoss with positive class weight 9.0
    (compensates for the $\sim$5\% tumour superpixel rate;
    class imbalance $\approx$19:1 background to tumour)
    \item \textbf{Optimiser}: AdamW \cite{loshchilov2019adamw},
    $\mathrm{lr} = 1\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $= 0.01$
    \item \textbf{Scheduler}: OneCycleLR, max\_lr $= 1\times10^{-3}$,
    \texttt{pct\_start} $= 0.3$
    \item \textbf{Batch}: 24 graphs per step; gradient accumulation steps $= 2$;
    \textbf{effective batch size 48}
    \item \textbf{Mixed precision}: AMP enabled
    \item \textbf{Regularisation}: Dropout 0.1 (fixed in model architecture),
    early stopping patience 10 on validation Dice
    \item \textbf{Epochs}: up to 50 per fold
    \item \textbf{Determinism}: \texttt{cudnn.benchmark=True},
    \texttt{cudnn.deterministic=False}; seed 42 applied to data splits only.
    Results are reproducible at the fold-assignment level but not at the weight level.
    \item \textbf{Hardware}: NVIDIA RTX 2060, 6\,GB VRAM
\end{itemize}

\textit{Note on loss function:} \texttt{CombinedLoss} (a BCE + Dice hybrid) was
defined in the codebase but was \textbf{never activated} in production training.
All five fold models use BCEWithLogitsLoss exclusively.

\subsection{Ensemble Inference}
\label{sec:ensemble}

Two complementary inference modes are defined for this system: \textbf{single-fold
inference} and \textbf{5-fold soft-voting ensemble inference}. Both use identical
model architecture and hyperparameters; they differ in the number of checkpoints
combined and how probability estimates are aggregated.

\subsubsection{Model 1: Single-Fold Inference}

In single-fold inference, a single fold-best-checkpoint model $\Phi_k$ is applied
independently to each graph $\mathcal{G}_j$. The per-node prediction is:
\[
    z_v^{(k)} = \mathbf{w}_k^\top \mathbf{h}_v^{(5)} + b_k,
    \quad
    P_k(v) = \sigma(z_v^{(k)}),
    \quad
    \hat{y}_k(v) = \mathbf{1}\!\left[P_k(v) \geq 0.5\right]
\]
where $\mathbf{h}_v^{(5)} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ is the node embedding at the fifth
GraphSAGE layer, $\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ and $b_k \in \mathbb{R}$ are
the classification head parameters of fold $k$, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid
function. Each of the five fold models $\Phi_1, \dots, \Phi_5$ is independently
trained on its respective 720-patient training partition; the fold-best checkpoint
is selected by maximum validation Dice. Single-fold mean cross-validation Dice:
\textbf{90.02\%\,$\pm$\,0.66\%}.

\subsubsection{Model 2: 5-Fold Soft-Voting Ensemble Inference}

In ensemble inference, all five fold-best checkpoints $\{\Phi_k\}_{k=1}^{5}$ are
applied to every graph $\mathcal{G}_j$. Each model produces an independent set of
per-node logits. The ensemble probability is the arithmetic mean of the five
sigmoid-transformed logits (soft voting):
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{ens}}(v) = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{5}
    \sigma\!\left(z_v^{(k)}\right),
    \quad
    \hat{y}_{\mathrm{ens}}(v) = \mathbf{1}\!\left[P_{\mathrm{ens}}(v) \geq 0.5\right]
    \label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}

Soft voting operates on calibrated probability estimates rather than hard binary
predictions, preserving uncertainty information from each fold. The averaging is
equivalent to Bayesian model averaging under a uniform prior over the five models.
Ensemble diversity arises from the different 720-patient training partitions in
each fold rather than from architectural or hyperparameter differences.

The patient-level Dice is computed by aggregating all per-graph node predictions
across the patient's $\approx$68 graph units, projecting predicted tumour nodes
to voxel space, and computing the Sørensen-Dice coefficient between the
reconstructed binary mask and the ground-truth annotation. Ensemble held-out Dice:
\textbf{91.41\%} --- a $+1.39$\,pp lift over single-fold inference
($p = 0.014$, one-sample $t$-test against the fold mean of 90.02\%).

\subsection{Data Integrity Validation}
\label{sec:integrity}

Fifteen automated checks are run before each training fold is initialised:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Feature dimension validation (15D per node)
    \item Patient-level train/val/test overlap detection across all fold pairs
    \item Label validity (binary \{0,1\})
    \item Per-modality normalisation range
    \item Data split seed reproducibility
    \item Graph structure validation (edge index bounds, node count consistency)
    \item Held-out set isolation (zero overlap with CV pool)
    \item Modality completeness (all 4 channels present)
    \item Slice count consistency per patient
    \item Superpixel count consistency per graph
    \item Split ratio validation (720/80/200)
    \item Checkpoint metadata validation
    \item Output tensor format verification
    \item Node feature leakage audit (no ground-truth-derived columns)
    \item Cross-fold patient disjointness verification
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Efficiency as a Structural Property}

The central result is that a 439K-parameter GNN trained on sparse superpixel graphs
matches or exceeds the accuracy of a 68M-parameter U-Net trained under identical
hardware constraints, while consuming 227$\times$ less GPU memory and running
5.9$\times$ faster end-to-end. This is not a training-trick result. The efficiency
advantage is \textit{structural}: it arises from the fundamental difference between
dense 3D convolution on 8.9M voxels and message-passing on $\approx$3,909 graph
nodes per patient. The parameter reduction, memory savings, and speedup hold
regardless of how well either model is individually tuned.

Three qualifications apply to this comparison. First, the 3D U-Net baseline is
trained with binary-only labels; published multi-class U-Net results (91--92\% WT
Dice) benefit from auxiliary gradient signal across ET/TC/WT sub-regions unavailable
in the binary formulation. A fully-tuned binary U-Net on larger hardware would likely
narrow the accuracy gap to near-zero, but would not alter the structural efficiency
advantages. Second, graph construction adds $\approx$1.66\,s overhead per patient
at first build time; in clinical batch-processing workflows, pre-cached graphs reduce
this to the 75.4\,ms inference-only figure. Third, the 15 handcrafted features encode
domain knowledge that must be adapted for tumour types outside gliomas.

\subsection{The Complete-Miss Failure Mode}

5\% of per-graph slices receive near-zero Dice scores, concentrated among patients
with atypical or absent T1CE enhancement. This failure mode is qualitatively
different from boundary imprecision: the model predicts all-background, resulting
in the patient being missed entirely rather than partially segmented. \textbf{Any
clinical deployment must include a post-processing gate that detects all-zero
output masks and flags them for mandatory radiologist review.} Addressing the root
cause requires adaptive superpixel granularity (finer regions near low-contrast
tumour boundaries), augmentation of atypical enhancement cases during training, or
learned feature representations that do not depend on strong T1CE contrast as the
primary discriminative signal.

\subsection{Deployment Implications}

The 75.4\,ms (pre-built graph) / 1.73\,s (end-to-end) inference times, 11\,MB peak
GPU memory, and 1.7\,MB model weight file collectively enable deployment scenarios
that are structurally infeasible for volumetric methods:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consumer-grade hardware.} Accurate inference on an RTX 2060
    (\$300--400) removes the hardware barrier for clinics in low- and middle-income
    countries that cannot procure A100-class GPUs (\$10,000+).
    \item \textbf{Edge and embedded deployment.} A 1.7\,MB model can be loaded onto
    embedded inference hardware for point-of-care or telemedicine devices.
    \item \textbf{High-throughput screening.} 11\,MB peak memory allows multiple
    concurrent inference processes on the same 6\,GB VRAM device, enabling batch
    screening workflows.
    \item \textbf{Interactive clinical use.} 75.4\,ms per-patient inference with
    pre-built graphs enables real-time feedback within a radiologist's review session.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We presented a superpixel-graph formulation for binary brain tumour segmentation
that achieves \textbf{91.41\% ensemble Dice} on a sealed 251-patient BraTS 2021
held-out set with \textbf{439,041 parameters}, \textbf{75.4\,ms} per-patient GNN
inference, and \textbf{11\,MB} peak GPU memory. Five-fold cross-validation confirms
\textbf{90.02\%\,$\pm$\,0.66\%} Dice with a 1.76\,pp inter-fold range. Zero-shot
transfer to BraTS 2023 yields \textbf{89.40\%} Dice (2.01\,pp gap, 1,245 patients),
demonstrating cross-edition robustness without any retraining. End-to-end processing
per patient requires \textbf{1,732\,ms} including on-the-fly SLIC graph construction,
a \textbf{5.9$\times$ speedup} over a hardware-matched 3D U-Net; for pre-cached
graphs, GNN inference takes \textbf{75.4\,ms}.

Systematic ablation on Fold 0 confirms the 5-layer, 256-dim GraphSAGE configuration
as Pareto-optimal under a 6\,GB VRAM constraint: added depth provides no accuracy
benefit, while the wider 512-dim and attention-based GAT variants trade efficiency
for modest gains at unacceptably higher parameter cost under this constraint. The
dominant failure mode---complete-miss predictions on patients with atypical T1CE
enhancement---is characterised, and a mandatory empty-prediction detection gate is
specified as a prerequisite for any clinical deployment.

These results demonstrate that graph-structured representations constitute a
practically deployable alternative to volumetric convolution for brain tumour
detection, enabling AI-assisted diagnostics on consumer-grade hardware where
3D CNN-based pipelines are not feasible.

\textbf{Limitations.}
This work addresses binary whole-tumour segmentation only; the 1.3--1.9\,pp gap
from multi-class SOTA methods (nnU-Net, Swin-UNETR) is partially attributable to
the absence of auxiliary ET/TC gradient signal. Cross-dataset evaluation is limited
to BraTS 2021$\rightarrow$2023; performance on non-glioma tumour types (meningioma,
metastases) and post-treatment imaging is untested. The 15 handcrafted node features
require domain expertise and may not generalise without modification to other tumour
types or scanner protocols.

\textbf{Future work.}
Immediate priorities: (1) multi-task extension for simultaneous ET/TC/WT prediction;
(2) GPU-accelerated SLIC to reduce end-to-end latency below 100\,ms; (3) adaptive
superpixel granularity to address complete-miss failures on low-contrast lesions;
and (4) prospective radiologist evaluation studies as a prerequisite for regulatory
pathway consideration.
