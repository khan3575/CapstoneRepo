% ---- Abstract ----
\begin{center}
    \section*{Abstract}
\end{center}

Brain tumor segmentation in multi-modal MRI remains computationally prohibitive for
real-time clinical deployment when approached through volumetric deep learning
pipelines. This paper presents a superpixel-graph formulation that reframes 3D MRI
segmentation as a node-classification problem on 2D spatial graphs, enabling
inference with a fraction of the memory and latency of comparable volumetric methods.
Each brain MRI volume is decomposed into a sequence of 2-slice graphs using SLIC
superpixels (up to 200 per slice; adaptively reduced to ${\sim}46$ on average based
on brain-region size), with 15-dimensional node features encoding multi-modal
intensity statistics, spatial coordinates, and morphological descriptors. A 5-layer
GraphSAGE network with 256-dimensional hidden layers (439,041 parameters total) is
trained end-to-end using BCEWithLogitsLoss on the BraTS 2021 dataset under a
5-fold patient-level cross-validation protocol. The model achieves a mean
cross-validation Dice of \textbf{90.02\%\,$\pm$\,0.66\%} across 1,000 patients and
an ensemble Dice of \textbf{91.41\%} on a sealed 251-patient held-out test set.
Zero-shot transfer to BraTS 2023 yields \textbf{89.40\% Dice} across 1,245 unseen
patients, a generalisation gap of 2.01 percentage points. End-to-end inference per
patient requires 1,732\,ms including on-the-fly graph construction; for pre-built
graphs, GNN inference alone runs in \textbf{75.4\,ms}, peaking at \textbf{11\,MB}
GPU memory --- 227 times less than a hardware-matched 3D U-Net baseline
benchmarked on identical hardware (RTX~2060, 6\,GB VRAM), yielding a
\textbf{5.9$\times$ end-to-end speedup}. These results suggest that
graph-structured representations provide a memory-efficient alternative to volumetric
convolution for binary tumor detection, with cross-dataset generalisation competitive
with methods that require full retraining.

\vspace{4mm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Brain Tumour Segmentation, Graph Neural Networks,
GraphSAGE, BraTS 2021, BraTS 2023, Computational Efficiency, Superpixel Graph,
Cross-Dataset Generalisation

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Brain tumour segmentation determines the margin between adequate and inadequate
oncological care. Before a neurosurgeon can plan a resection, before a radiation
oncologist can delineate a target volume, and before a neuro-oncologist can assess
treatment response, the tumour boundary must be traced with sub-millimetre precision
across multiple MRI modalities. Manual delineation by a trained radiologist requires
90 to 120 minutes per case \cite{menze2015multimodal}; for a centre processing 40
cases per week, this represents 60 to 80 radiologist-hours devoted entirely to
contouring. Automated segmentation has therefore been a sustained research priority
for two decades.

The Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) challenge \cite{menze2015multimodal, bakas2021brats}
has anchored this priority to a reproducible benchmark. Annually updated datasets of
expert-annotated glioma cases---covering T1-weighted (T1), contrast-enhanced T1
(T1CE), T2-weighted (T2), and Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR)
modalities---have driven successive generations of model development. The leading
current methods are volumetric convolutional networks: nnU-Net
\cite{isensee2021nnunet} achieves $\sim$92.7\% Whole Tumour (WT) Dice on BraTS 2021
at 31 million parameters; Swin-UNETR \cite{hatamizadeh2022swin} reaches 93.3\% at 62
million parameters. These numbers are clinically meaningful.

The obstacle is the infrastructure they require. nnU-Net's auto-configured training
pipeline demands 32--40\,GB VRAM (V100/A100-class hardware); even at inference,
volumetric 3D models consume multiple gigabytes of GPU memory with per-patient
processing times of 10 to 30 seconds. For the majority of hospitals globally where
such hardware is absent, state-of-the-art brain tumour AI is effectively
unavailable.

We identified a structural mismatch at the root of this problem. Brain tumours occupy
on average 5--10\% of total brain volume. Volumetric CNNs nonetheless process every
voxel in the complete 3D grid---dense computation on a spatially sparse signal. The
computational cost scales with the full volume, not with the tumour. Graph Neural
Networks \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} address this mismatch by design: rather than
voxels, a GNN operates on superpixel nodes---spatially compact clusters of adjacent
voxels produced by SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic}. This collapses the spatial
representation by orders of magnitude while preserving the multi-modal intensity
patterns that discriminate tumour from healthy tissue.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Given the clinical burden described above, automated segmentation must be both
accurate and computationally accessible. The best automated methods --- nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet}, Swin-UNETR
\cite{hatamizadeh2022swin} --- use 3D convolutions that process the entire MRI
volume as a dense grid. Every voxel in a $240 \times 240 \times 155$ volume
receives the same computation, whether it belongs to the tumour or to healthy
tissue. Gliomas, however, typically make up only 5--10\% of the brain volume.
The remaining 90\% or more is healthy tissue that the model must process anyway,
because convolution treats every voxel the same. The computational cost therefore
scales with the full volume, not with the tumour. In practice, this means models with 31--62 million parameters
\cite{isensee2021nnunet, hatamizadeh2022swin} that require multiple gigabytes
of GPU memory and per-patient processing times measured in seconds to minutes
on high-end hardware. Most hospitals do not have that hardware.

Graph neural networks offer a way around this. Instead of processing every voxel,
a GNN operates on superpixels --- groups of similar neighbouring pixels produced
by algorithms like SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic}. A typical BraTS slice reduces
from $240 \times 240 = 57{,}600$ voxels to roughly 46 superpixel nodes on average
(the SLIC target of 200 is adaptively lowered based on the brain-region area of each
slice). The graph
captures which regions are next to each other, and each node carries a feature
vector summarising the intensity and spatial properties of its region. This
representation is far smaller than the original volume, but it keeps the
information the model needs to tell tumour from non-tumour tissue apart. Yet
existing GNN work in brain tumour segmentation has not been tested at scale:
studies use small patient sets, operate on individual 2D slices without
cross-slice connectivity, and do not report controlled efficiency comparisons
against CNN baselines on the same hardware \cite{kipf2017gcn, hamilton2017graphsage}.
No published work evaluates a GNN ensemble on the full BraTS 2021 dataset under a
sealed held-out protocol with end-to-end timing benchmarks.

This work asks a direct question: can a superpixel-based graph neural network
segment brain tumours from multi-modal 3D MRI with accuracy competitive to
volumetric CNNs, while using a fraction of the parameters, memory, and inference
time? Answering this requires training a competitive model, then validating it
through patient-level cross-validation, sealed held-out evaluation, and controlled
benchmarking --- none of which has been done for graph-based brain tumour
segmentation before.

\subsection{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

This research targets four measurable objectives, each verifiable against
the experimental results in Section~\ref{sec:results}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Graph construction pipeline.} Build a preprocessing
    pipeline that converts each 4-modality BraTS 2021 MRI volume into a
    set of superpixel graphs using SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic}, with
    15-dimensional node features encoding intensity statistics, spatial
    position, and region shape. The pipeline must produce zero
    ground-truth label leakage, verified programmatically before each
    training fold.

    \item \textbf{Competitive GNN segmentation.} Train a GraphSAGE-based
    \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} binary node classifier with fewer than
    500\,K trainable parameters to achieve mean Dice $\geq 90\%$ on a
    sealed 251-patient held-out test set under 5-fold patient-level
    cross-validation across 1,000 training patients. A soft-voting
    ensemble of the five fold-best models should target Dice above 91\%,
    within reach of leading volumetric CNNs on the same benchmark.

    \item \textbf{Efficiency benchmarking.} Measure end-to-end
    per-patient inference time, peak GPU memory, parameter count, and
    model size on disk for both the proposed GNN and a 3D U-Net baseline
    \cite{cicek20163d} trained on the same data, using identical
    hardware. The GNN must demonstrate at least a $5\times$ speedup over
    the baseline in end-to-end inference.

    \item \textbf{Cross-edition generalisation.} Evaluate the trained
    ensemble on all 1,245 BraTS 2023 patients without any retraining or
    threshold adjustment, and measure the Dice gap relative to BraTS 2021
    held-out performance. A gap under 5 percentage points confirms that
    the model generalises across dataset editions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

GNN-based brain tumour segmentation has not been evaluated at the scale
or rigour that the BraTS benchmark demands. Three specific gaps separate
existing GNN work from a credible comparison with volumetric CNNs.

\textbf{No ensemble evaluation.} Soft-voting ensembles are standard
practice in volumetric CNN pipelines. nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet}
trains five folds and averages their predictions; the resulting ensemble
typically improves Dice by 1--3 percentage points over any single fold.
BraTS leaderboard rankings reflect ensemble results, not single-model
results, so any method claiming competitive accuracy must be evaluated
the same way. No published work trains a multi-fold GNN on BraTS and
reports ensemble Dice on a sealed held-out set. Without this, comparing
a single-fold GNN to an ensemble CNN is not a fair comparison.

\textbf{No controlled efficiency measurement.} The practical argument
for graph-based segmentation is efficiency: fewer parameters, less
memory, faster inference. But this argument requires measurement, not
assumption. Saueressig et al.\ \cite{saueressig2021exploring} apply
GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT to BraTS~2019 supervoxel graphs and claim
``5 to 15 times faster'' training than CNNs, yet report no inference
time, no GPU memory usage, and no parameter count. Patel et
al.\ \cite{patel2023multiclass} train a GAT on BraTS~2021 with 10.2M
parameters and measure 1.7\,s average inference --- but on a Tesla K80,
with no CNN baseline tested on the same device. Neither study runs a
controlled comparison where both a GNN and a CNN are benchmarked on
identical hardware under identical conditions. Without such a
comparison, the efficiency advantage of GNNs over CNNs for brain tumour
segmentation remains a hypothesis, not a result.

\textbf{No cross-edition generalisation test.} BraTS releases a new
dataset edition each year with different patients, institutions, and
sometimes different annotation protocols \cite{menze2015multimodal,
bakas2021brats}. Standard CNN pipelines retrain per edition
\cite{isensee2021nnunet}. Whether a superpixel-based GNN trained on one
edition can segment patients from a different edition --- without
retraining or threshold adjustment --- has not been tested. This is a
direct test of whether the graph representation captures features that
transfer across acquisition differences, or whether it overfits to the
training data.

This work addresses all three gaps: a 5-fold soft-voting ensemble
evaluated on a sealed 251-patient held-out set; controlled benchmarking
of inference time, memory, parameters, and model size against a 3D U-Net
\cite{cicek20163d} baseline on identical hardware; and zero-shot
evaluation on 1,245 BraTS 2023 patients with no retraining.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

We make five contributions, each addressing one or more of the gaps identified
above:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Superpixel-graph pipeline.} A preprocessing pipeline that
    converts each 3D BraTS volume into a sequence of paired-slice superpixel
    graphs using SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic}. Each node carries a
    15-dimensional feature vector encoding intensity statistics from all four
    MRI sequences, normalised spatial coordinates, and superpixel area.
    Ground-truth labels are excluded from all node features to prevent
    data leakage.

    \item \textbf{GraphSAGE binary classifier.} A 5-layer GraphSAGE
    \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} network with 439,041 parameters---157$\times$
    smaller than the 3D U-Net baseline---trained under 5-fold patient-level
    cross-validation. Mean held-out Dice:
    \textbf{90.02\%\,$\pm$\,0.66\%} across 1,000 patients.

    \item \textbf{5-fold soft-voting ensemble.} Soft-voting aggregation of
    five fold-best checkpoints achieves \textbf{91.41\% Dice} on a sealed
    251-patient held-out set---a $+1.39$\,pp lift over the single-fold mean
    and the primary evaluation result of this paper.

    \item \textbf{Zero-shot cross-edition evaluation.} Direct evaluation on
    BraTS 2023 (1,245 patients, no retraining) yields \textbf{89.40\% Dice},
    a 2.01\,pp generalisation gap that confirms cross-edition robustness
    without domain adaptation.

    \item \textbf{Controlled efficiency benchmarking.} On identical hardware
    (RTX~2060), GNN inference on pre-built graphs runs in
    \textbf{75.4\,ms} at \textbf{11\,MB} peak GPU memory per patient.
    End-to-end including on-the-fly graph construction:
    \textbf{1,732\,ms}---a \textbf{5.9$\times$ speedup} over a 3D U-Net
    \cite{cicek20163d} baseline, with \textbf{157$\times$ fewer parameters}
    and \textbf{227$\times$ lower peak GPU memory}.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:discussion} addresses efficiency trade-offs, failure modes, and

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Volumetric CNN Methods for Brain Tumour Segmentation}

Convolutional neural networks have dominated medical image segmentation since
the mid-2010s, with a comprehensive survey by Litjens et al.\
\cite{litjens2017survey} documenting their rapid adoption across radiology,
pathology, and ophthalmology. In brain tumour segmentation specifically, early
CNN approaches operated on 2D slices. Pereira et al.\ \cite{pereira2016brain}
demonstrated that deep CNNs trained on individual MRI slices could outperform
handcrafted features for glioma segmentation, but slice-by-slice processing
discards the volumetric context that is critical for capturing the 3D morphology
of brain tumours.

The original 2D U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} introduced the symmetric
encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections that has become the foundation
of nearly all subsequent segmentation networks. The encoder progressively
downsamples the input through convolutional and pooling layers to capture
high-level semantic features, while the decoder upsamples to recover spatial
resolution. Skip connections concatenate encoder feature maps with the
corresponding decoder layers, allowing the network to combine coarse semantic
information with fine-grained spatial detail. This architecture was initially
designed for biomedical microscopy images and trained with as few as 30 annotated
samples, demonstrating data-efficient learning that proved crucial for medical
imaging applications where annotated data is scarce.

The 3D U-Net \cite{cicek20163d} extended this architecture to volumetric data by
replacing all 2D convolutions with their 3D counterparts. Instead of processing
individual slices, 3D U-Net operates on entire sub-volumes, applying
$3 \times 3 \times 3$ convolutional kernels that capture spatial relationships
along all three axes simultaneously. This allows the network to learn features
that span multiple slices --- for instance, the tubular shape of a tumour
infiltrating across axial planes --- which 2D networks cannot represent. Skip
connections in 3D U-Net work analogously to their 2D counterparts, concatenating
volumetric feature maps from the encoder to the decoder at each resolution level.
The key trade-off is computational: 3D convolutions on a
$240 \times 240 \times 155$ volume require substantially more memory and
computation than processing the same volume as 155 independent 2D slices.

V-Net \cite{milletari2016vnet} made two important contributions beyond the 3D
U-Net architecture. First, it introduced residual connections within each encoder
and decoder stage, allowing gradients to flow more easily through the deep network.
Second, and more significantly for the field, it proposed the Dice loss function
as a training objective for segmentation. The Dice loss directly optimises the
overlap between predicted and ground-truth masks, making it inherently robust to
class imbalance --- a critical property in brain tumour segmentation where tumour
voxels typically constitute only 5--10\% of the brain volume. The Dice loss became
a standard component in subsequent segmentation pipelines, often combined with
cross-entropy in hybrid loss formulations.

Attention U-Net \cite{oktay2018attention} introduced learnable soft attention
gates into the skip connections of the U-Net architecture. Rather than
concatenating encoder features to the decoder indiscriminately, attention gates
learn to weight spatial regions based on their relevance to the segmentation
task. The gating mechanism suppresses feature activations in irrelevant
background regions and amplifies responses in regions likely to contain the
target anatomy. This is particularly beneficial for brain tumour segmentation,
where the tumour occupies a small and spatially variable fraction of the total
volume; attention gates allow the decoder to focus computational resources on
the tumour-bearing regions rather than processing the entire volume uniformly.

UNet++ \cite{zhou2018unetpp} redesigned the skip connections as dense nested
pathways, where intermediate convolutional blocks bridge the semantic gap
between encoder and decoder feature maps at each resolution level. This dense
connectivity reduces the feature discrepancy across the skip connections and
allows the network to capture features at multiple semantic scales. While UNet++
demonstrated improved segmentation accuracy on several medical imaging benchmarks,
the additional convolutional blocks increase both parameter count and inference
time relative to the original U-Net.

Kamnitsas et al.\ \cite{kamnitsas2017efficient} proposed DeepMedic, a
multi-scale 3D CNN that processes the input volume at two resolutions
simultaneously: a high-resolution pathway captures fine boundary detail, while
a low-resolution pathway provides broader spatial context. A fully connected
conditional random field (CRF) post-processing step refines the segmentation
boundaries. This multi-scale approach achieved state-of-the-art results on the
BraTS 2015 challenge and demonstrated that combining local and global context
is essential for accurate tumour boundary delineation.

nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet} represented a paradigm shift by automating
the entire segmentation pipeline. Rather than requiring manual tuning of
preprocessing steps, patch sizes, network topology, and training schedules,
nnU-Net analyses dataset ``fingerprints'' --- volume dimensions, voxel spacing,
class distribution, and dataset size --- to automatically configure all pipeline
parameters. It selects between 2D, 3D full-resolution, and 3D cascade
architectures based on the dataset characteristics, and applies standardised
preprocessing including resampling, intensity normalisation, and data
augmentation. On BraTS 2021, nnU-Net achieves approximately 92.7\% Whole
Tumour Dice at 31 million parameters. Its success demonstrated that systematic
engineering of the training pipeline is as important as architectural innovation,
and it has become the de facto baseline for medical image segmentation benchmarks.

The introduction of Vision Transformers \cite{dosovitskiy2021image} to image
recognition opened a new direction for segmentation architectures. TransBTS
\cite{wang2021transbts} was among the first to integrate Transformer encoders
into a 3D U-Net backbone for brain tumour segmentation. The Transformer module
operates on the bottleneck features of the CNN encoder, capturing long-range
spatial dependencies that convolutional kernels with limited receptive fields
cannot model directly. By attending to all spatial positions simultaneously,
the Transformer enables the network to relate distant tumour regions ---
for example, satellite lesions separated from the main tumour mass --- that
would require many stacked convolutional layers to connect. TransBTS achieved
90.1\% WT Dice on BraTS 2021 with 33 million parameters.

Swin-UNETR \cite{hatamizadeh2022swin} advanced this direction further by
replacing the CNN encoder entirely with a hierarchical Swin Transformer
\cite{liu2021swin}. The Swin Transformer divides the input volume into
non-overlapping windows and computes self-attention within each window,
then shifts the window partition to enable cross-window information exchange.
This shifted-window mechanism reduces the quadratic complexity of standard
self-attention to linear complexity with respect to volume size, making
Transformer-based encoding computationally feasible for 3D medical volumes.
Swin-UNETR reaches 93.3\% WT Dice on BraTS 2021 at 62 million parameters,
representing the current state of the art on this benchmark. Myronenko
\cite{myronenko2019brats}, who won the BraTS 2018 challenge, demonstrated
that adding a variational autoencoder branch to the decoder provides
regularisation that further improves segmentation quality, achieving top
performance with a 3D encoder-decoder architecture.

A defining characteristic of all these methods is that inference memory and
latency scale with the 3D volume resolution, regardless of how much of that
volume actually contains tumour. Reported GPU memory requirements range from
2.5\,GB (nnU-Net) to over 6\,GB (Swin-UNETR); per-patient inference times on
high-end hardware are 10 to 30 seconds. On the RTX 2060 (6\,GB VRAM) used in
this work, a binary 3D U-Net consumes the full memory budget at $\sim$10.2
seconds per patient. The computational bottleneck is therefore not accuracy ---
these methods are clinically accurate --- but the hardware barrier to deployment.
For the majority of hospitals globally, particularly in low- and middle-income
countries, the GPU hardware required to run these models is either unavailable
or prohibitively expensive, creating a gap between research capability and
clinical accessibility.

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks in Medical Imaging}

Graph neural networks (GNNs) extend deep learning from regular grids
(images, volumes) to irregular, non-Euclidean structures such as molecular
graphs, social networks, and point clouds. The foundational GNN model was
introduced by Scarselli et al.\ \cite{scarselli2009graph}, who proposed
iterative message passing between nodes until a fixed-point equilibrium is
reached. While theoretically general, this early formulation was computationally
expensive and difficult to train, limiting its practical adoption.

The modern era of GNNs began with spectral approaches. Defferrard et al.\
\cite{defferrard2016convolutional} introduced ChebNet, which defines graph
convolutions as polynomial filters on the graph Laplacian eigenspectrum,
approximated using Chebyshev polynomials. This avoids the expensive eigenvalue
decomposition required by exact spectral methods and allows localised filters
of controllable size. Kipf and Welling \cite{kipf2017gcn} simplified ChebNet
into Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) by restricting the Chebyshev polynomial
to first order, yielding a layer-wise propagation rule where each node aggregates
features from its immediate neighbours through a symmetric normalised adjacency
matrix. GCNs achieved competitive semi-supervised classification results on
citation networks and became the most widely used GNN variant. However, GCNs
are \textit{transductive}: they require the entire graph structure to be known
at training time, which limits their applicability to settings where new nodes
appear at inference time --- precisely the scenario in medical imaging, where
each new patient produces a previously unseen graph.

GraphSAGE \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} addressed this limitation by introducing
an \textit{inductive} learning framework. Instead of learning fixed node
embeddings tied to a specific graph structure, GraphSAGE learns an aggregation
function that generates embeddings by sampling and aggregating features from a
node's local neighbourhood. Three aggregation strategies were proposed: mean
pooling (averaging neighbour features), max pooling (element-wise maximum), and
LSTM-based aggregation (processing neighbours through a recurrent network). The
mean-pooling variant concatenates the aggregated neighbourhood representation
with the node's own features and transforms the result through a learnable weight
matrix, producing the updated node embedding. This inductive design means that a
model trained on one set of graphs can be applied directly to entirely new graphs
at inference time --- a property that is essential for medical imaging, where each
patient produces a unique graph that was never seen during training.

Graph Attention Networks (GAT) \cite{velickovic2018gat} introduced a
self-attention mechanism into the message-passing framework. Rather than
treating all neighbours equally (as in GCN) or sampling uniformly (as in
GraphSAGE mean pooling), GAT computes attention coefficients that assign
different importance weights to different neighbours. The attention mechanism
uses a shared linear transformation followed by a single-layer feedforward
network to compute pairwise attention scores, which are then normalised using
the softmax function. Multi-head attention further stabilises training by
computing $K$ independent attention heads and concatenating (or averaging)
their outputs. While GAT offers greater expressiveness, the attention
computation increases parameter count and memory usage proportionally to the
number of heads, creating a trade-off between model capacity and efficiency.

The message-passing framework unifying these approaches was formalised by
Gilmer et al.\ \cite{gilmer2017neural} as Message Passing Neural Networks
(MPNNs). In this framework, each GNN layer consists of two phases: a
\textit{message} phase, where each node collects information from its
neighbours through a learnable message function; and an \textit{update}
phase, where the node's hidden state is updated based on the aggregated
messages. GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT are all instances of this general framework,
differing only in how they define the message and update functions. Wu et al.\
\cite{wu2021comprehensive} provide a comprehensive taxonomy of GNN architectures,
categorising them into spectral methods, spatial methods, and attention-based
methods, and surveying their applications across domains.

Point cloud methods represent a parallel development in non-grid deep learning.
PointNet \cite{qi2017pointnet} introduced learnable aggregation over
unordered 3D point sets using shared multilayer perceptrons followed by a
symmetric max-pooling function, achieving permutation invariance without
requiring an explicit graph structure. While PointNet operates on raw point
coordinates rather than graph edges, it established the broader principle that
deep learning can be applied to irregular spatial structures by designing
architectures that respect the structure's inherent symmetries.

In medical imaging, GNNs have been applied across several domains. Parisot
et al.\ \cite{parisot2017spectral} constructed population graphs where each
node represents a patient and edges encode phenotypic similarity (age, sex,
acquisition site), then applied spectral graph convolutions for disease
classification in Alzheimer's and autism spectrum disorder cohorts. This work
demonstrated that GNNs can capture inter-subject relationships that are
invisible to methods processing each patient independently. In histopathology,
tissue sections are naturally represented as graphs: cells or tissue patches
form nodes, and spatial adjacency defines edges. GNN-based classification of
such tissue graphs has shown competitive performance for grading and subtyping
tasks \cite{jha2021instance}. In neuroimaging, GNNs have been applied to
brain connectome analysis, where nodes represent brain regions and edges
encode structural or functional connectivity \cite{bessadok2022graph}.

In the brain tumour domain specifically, Saueressig et al.\
\cite{saueressig2021exploring} applied GCN, GraphSAGE, and GAT architectures
to BraTS 2019, converting MRI volumes into supervoxel graphs using 3D SLIC.
They reported Dice scores between 82\% and 86\% and claimed training speedups
of 5 to 15 times relative to CNN baselines. However, their evaluation was
limited to a small patient subset, used only 3D supervoxels without
investigating 2D superpixel alternatives, and did not report inference time,
GPU memory, or parameter counts --- making it impossible to verify the
efficiency claims. Patel et al.\ \cite{patel2023multiclass} trained a GAT
on BraTS 2021 for multi-class segmentation with 10.2 million parameters,
reporting a mean Dice of 84.3\% and an average inference time of 1.7 seconds.
Their evaluation was conducted on a Tesla K80 GPU, and no CNN baseline was
tested on the same hardware, preventing controlled efficiency comparison.

Despite these initial explorations, no published work has evaluated a
GNN-based brain tumour segmentation pipeline at the scale and rigour that the
BraTS benchmark demands. Application to 3D multi-modal MRI segmentation with
rigorous patient-level cross-validation, data-integrity auditing, and efficiency
benchmarking against volumetric baselines on identical hardware remains
unaddressed. No published work with a directly comparable binary whole-tumour
BraTS 2021 experimental protocol was identified at the time of submission.
This work is, to our knowledge, the first systematic efficiency analysis of
GNN-based binary brain tumour segmentation on BraTS.

\subsection{Superpixel-Based Representations}

Superpixels are mid-level image representations that group perceptually similar,
spatially contiguous pixels into compact regions. The motivation for superpixels
is twofold: they reduce the number of primitives that subsequent algorithms must
process (from hundreds of thousands of pixels to hundreds or thousands of
regions), and they align region boundaries with natural image edges, providing a
more semantically meaningful unit of analysis than raw pixels or fixed-grid
patches.

Early superpixel methods were rooted in graph-based image segmentation.
Normalised Cuts \cite{shi2000normalized} formulated image segmentation as a
graph partitioning problem, where pixels are nodes, edges encode pairwise
similarity, and the optimal partition minimises a normalised cut criterion that
balances within-cluster similarity against between-cluster dissimilarity. While
theoretically principled, Normalised Cuts requires computing eigenvectors of
the graph Laplacian, resulting in $O(n^{3/2})$ complexity that is prohibitive
for high-resolution images. Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher
\cite{felzenszwalb2004efficient} proposed a more efficient graph-based method
that merges regions greedily based on a minimum spanning tree, running in
$O(n \log n)$ time. This method produces segments that adapt to image content
but with irregular sizes and shapes, which can be undesirable when uniform
spatial coverage is needed.

SLIC (Simple Linear Iterative Clustering) \cite{achanta2012slic} addressed
these limitations by combining the efficiency of $k$-means clustering with
spatial regularisation. The algorithm initialises $K$ cluster centres on a
regular grid and iteratively assigns each pixel to the nearest cluster centre
in a 5-dimensional space that combines colour (or intensity) channels with
normalised spatial coordinates $(x, y)$. The key design insight is that each
cluster centre only searches within a $2S \times 2S$ neighbourhood (where
$S = \sqrt{N/K}$ is the grid spacing), reducing the complexity from $O(NKI)$
for standard $k$-means to $O(NI)$, where $N$ is the number of pixels and $I$
is the number of iterations. A compactness parameter $m$ controls the trade-off
between colour homogeneity and spatial regularity: higher values produce more
regularly shaped superpixels at the cost of weaker boundary adherence, while
lower values allow superpixels to deform along intensity edges.

Stutz et al.\ \cite{stutz2018superpixels} conducted an extensive evaluation of
superpixel algorithms on multiple benchmarks, comparing SLIC against
graph-based, watershed-based, and energy-based alternatives. SLIC consistently
ranked among the top methods in terms of the trade-off between boundary recall
(how well superpixel boundaries align with true object edges), under-segmentation
error (how much object area leaks across superpixel boundaries), and
computational speed. The evaluation confirmed that SLIC's combination of
$O(N)$ runtime, controllable segment count, compact spatial coverage, and
strong boundary adherence makes it the most practical superpixel algorithm for
pipelines where consistent spatial coverage and computational predictability
are required.

In medical imaging, superpixel-based representations have been applied across
several domains. In retinal image analysis, superpixels group vessel and
background pixels into compact regions that serve as input to classifiers for
vessel segmentation and lesion detection \cite{orlando2017retinal}. In
histopathology, tissue sections are divided into superpixel regions that
capture local cell density, staining intensity, and texture patterns, which
are then classified individually or connected into tissue graphs for GNN-based
analysis \cite{jha2021instance}. In cardiac MRI, superpixels reduce the
spatial resolution of short-axis slices while preserving myocardial boundary
information, enabling efficient segmentation of aortic image sequences
\cite{bai2018aortic}.

Adapting SLIC to 3D multi-modal MRI introduces non-trivial challenges absent
from 2D applications. First, inter-slice connectivity must be explicitly
modelled, because SLIC operates within individual 2D planes and does not
natively produce connections between superpixels in adjacent slices. In
volumetric MRI, anatomical structures extend continuously across slices, and a
segmentation model must propagate information along this third dimension to make
spatially coherent predictions. Second, normalisation must account for
modality-specific intensity ranges: the four MRI sequences (T1, T1CE, T2,
FLAIR) each have different intensity distributions and tissue contrast
properties, and the superpixel features must fuse information from all four
channels in a way that is invariant to inter-patient intensity variation.
Third, feature engineering must incorporate clinically relevant information ---
intensity statistics, spatial position, and region morphology --- without
including ground-truth labels, which would constitute data leakage and
invalidate the evaluation. Our pipeline addresses all three challenges, as
detailed in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\subsection{MRI Modalities and Their Clinical Significance}
\label{sec:modalities}

The BraTS dataset provides four co-registered MRI sequences for each patient,
each acquired using a different pulse sequence that produces distinct tissue
contrast. Understanding what each modality reveals is essential for interpreting
why the pipeline fuses information from all four channels into each superpixel
node feature.

\textbf{T1-weighted (T1)} imaging uses a short repetition time (TR) and short
echo time (TE) to produce contrast based primarily on the longitudinal
relaxation properties of tissues. In T1 images, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)
appears dark, white matter appears bright, and grey matter appears intermediate.
T1 provides excellent anatomical detail and is the standard sequence for
structural brain imaging, allowing clear delineation of cortical folds,
subcortical nuclei, and ventricles. Tumour tissue typically appears
hypointense (darker than surrounding brain) on T1 due to increased water
content from oedema and cellular disruption.

\textbf{T1-weighted Contrast-Enhanced (T1CE)} is acquired after intravenous
administration of a gadolinium-based contrast agent. Gadolinium shortens the
T1 relaxation time of nearby water molecules, causing enhancement (brightening)
in regions where the contrast agent accumulates. In brain tumours, the
blood-brain barrier is disrupted in actively growing tumour regions, allowing
gadolinium to leak into the extravascular space and produce bright enhancement.
The enhancing region on T1CE corresponds to the most metabolically active
portion of the tumour and is used to define the Enhancing Tumour (ET)
sub-region in BraTS annotations. T1CE provides the strongest signal for
distinguishing active tumour from surrounding tissue and is the primary
channel used for SLIC superpixel clustering in our pipeline.

\textbf{T2-weighted (T2)} imaging uses a long TR and long TE, producing
contrast based on transverse relaxation properties. CSF appears bright,
grey matter is brighter than white matter, and regions with increased water
content --- including both tumour tissue and surrounding oedema --- appear
hyperintense (brighter than normal brain). T2 is particularly sensitive to
peritumoral oedema, the swelling of brain tissue around the tumour caused by
fluid accumulation. This makes T2 valuable for delineating the full extent of
tumour-affected tissue, including regions beyond the enhancing tumour core.

\textbf{Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR)} is a modified T2
sequence that suppresses the bright CSF signal using an inversion pulse.
By nulling CSF, FLAIR makes periventricular and cortical lesions --- which
would otherwise be obscured by adjacent bright CSF on standard T2 --- much
more conspicuous. In brain tumour imaging, FLAIR highlights the full extent
of the Whole Tumour (WT) region, including non-enhancing tumour infiltration
and peritumoral oedema, which appear bright against the suppressed CSF
background. FLAIR is considered the most informative single modality for
whole-tumour delineation.

The four modalities are complementary: T1 provides anatomical structure, T1CE
highlights active tumour, T2 reveals oedema and fluid, and FLAIR delineates
the full tumour extent with CSF suppression. By extracting intensity statistics
(mean and standard deviation) from all four modalities within each superpixel,
our node features encode a multi-contrast tissue signature that captures the
discriminative information needed to distinguish tumour from healthy tissue
\cite{menze2015multimodal, louis2016glioma}.

\subsection{Proposed Model and Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

The proposed system converts 3D multi-modal MRI volumes into superpixel
graphs and performs tumour segmentation as a node classification task.
Instead of processing every voxel with 3D convolutions, the pipeline
operates on $\approx$92 superpixel nodes per graph---each representing a
cluster of similar neighbouring pixels---reducing both memory and
computation by orders of magnitude.

Formally, the pipeline implements the transformation:
\[
    \hat{M} = \Phi\!\left(\mathcal{G}(V)\right),
    \quad V \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 240 \times 240 \times 155},
    \quad \hat{M} \in \{0,1\}^{240 \times 240 \times 155}
\]
where $V$ is the four-modality (T1, T1CE, T2, FLAIR) MRI volume,
$\mathcal{G}(\cdot)$ is the graph construction operator, and
$\Phi(\cdot)$ is the trained GraphSAGE classifier. The pipeline has four
stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Volumetric preprocessing.} Per-modality Z-score
    normalisation within the skull-stripped brain mask; active axial slice
    selection (brain-fraction threshold $\tau = 0.05$); grouping
    consecutive active slices into non-overlapping 2-slice units
    ($\approx$69 per patient).

    \item \textbf{Superpixel graph construction.} SLIC
    \cite{achanta2012slic} superpixel segmentation on each 2-slice unit
    ($K = 200$ target segments, $\approx$46 realised per slice);
    15-dimensional node feature extraction from all four MRI modalities;
    edge construction via Region Adjacency Graph (RAG) within slices and
    $k$-nearest-neighbour matching across slices.

    \item \textbf{GraphSAGE node classification.} A 5-layer GraphSAGE
    \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} network with 256-dimensional hidden
    channels (439,041 total parameters) classifies each node as tumour
    or non-tumour independently per graph.

    \item \textbf{Ensemble inference and reconstruction.} Five fold-best
    checkpoints produce per-node predictions via soft-voting; predicted
    tumour nodes are back-projected to voxel space to reconstruct the
    binary mask $\hat{M}$.
\end{enumerate}

The graph representation compresses each patient from 8,928,000 voxels to
$\approx$3,909 nodes---a $2{,}284\times$ reduction
(Section~\ref{sec:graphconst}). On an NVIDIA RTX~2060, end-to-end inference
takes 1,732\,ms per patient; with pre-built graphs, GNN inference alone
runs in 75.4\,ms at 11\,MB peak GPU memory.
Figure~\ref{fig:proposed_model} illustrates the complete pipeline and the
internal GraphSAGE architecture.

\subsection{Mathematical Description of the Pipeline}
\label{sec:mathpipeline}

\textbf{Step 1 --- Per-modality Z-score normalisation.}
MRI scanners from different manufacturers and institutions produce images with
different absolute intensity scales. Unlike CT imaging, where Hounsfield units
provide a standardised scale, MRI intensities are arbitrary and vary between
scanners, acquisition protocols, and even between sessions on the same scanner.
This means that the same tissue type may have an intensity of 500 in one patient's
scan and 1200 in another, making raw intensities unsuitable as direct input to a
machine learning model. Z-score normalisation addresses this by transforming each
patient's intensities to have zero mean and unit variance, so that the model
learns tissue contrast patterns rather than absolute intensity values.

Let $V_m \in \mathbb{R}^{240 \times 240 \times 155}$ denote the volume for modality
$m \in \{\text{T1}, \text{T1CE}, \text{T2}, \text{FLAIR}\}$ and let
$\mathcal{B} \subset \{1,\dots,240\}^2 \times \{1,\dots,155\}$ be the set of voxel
indices within the skull-stripped brain mask. The normalised volume is:
\[
    \tilde{V}_m(i) = \frac{V_m(i) - \mu_m}{\sigma_m},
    \quad i \in \mathcal{B}
\]
where $\mu_m = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} V_m(i)$ and
$\sigma_m = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}}(V_m(i)-\mu_m)^2}$
are the intra-patient, within-mask mean and standard deviation for modality $m$.
Normalisation is computed within the brain mask only, excluding background
(air) voxels that would otherwise skew the statistics with a large mass of zero
values. Each modality is normalised independently because T1, T1CE, T2, and FLAIR
have fundamentally different intensity ranges and tissue contrasts. No
inter-patient normalisation is applied; the Z-score transform is sufficient to
standardise the within-patient intensity distribution for each modality.

\textbf{Step 2 --- Active slice selection.}
Not all 155 axial slices in a BraTS volume contain meaningful brain tissue.
The top and bottom slices of the volume often consist entirely or almost
entirely of background (empty space above the skull and below the brainstem).
Processing these empty slices would waste computation by creating superpixel
graphs with no informative content. Active slice selection removes these
uninformative slices by applying a simple brain-fraction threshold.

A slice $z \in \{1,\dots,155\}$ is classified as active if the fraction of
non-zero brain-mask voxels in that slice exceeds a threshold $\tau = 0.05$:
\[
    z \text{ is active} \iff
    \frac{|\{(x,y) : (x,y,z) \in \mathcal{B}\}|}{240 \times 240} \geq \tau
\]
The threshold $\tau = 0.05$ (5\% of the slice area) is deliberately
conservative: it retains all slices with any substantial brain content while
excluding only those that are nearly or completely empty. This ensures that no
tumour-bearing slice is inadvertently discarded, since even peripheral tumour
regions occupy more than 5\% of their slice.

Consecutive active slices are grouped into non-overlapping 2-slice units
$\mathcal{S}_j = \{z_{2j-1},\, z_{2j}\}$. The 2-slice grouping serves two
purposes. First, it introduces minimal cross-slice context within each graph,
allowing the model to learn spatial relationships between adjacent slices
without requiring full volumetric processing. Second, it keeps each graph
small ($\approx$92 nodes) and uniform in size, which simplifies batching
during training and ensures predictable memory usage. The empirically measured
mean is $137.5 \pm 6.9$ active slices per patient, yielding $\approx$68--69
graph units per patient.

\textbf{Step 3 --- SLIC superpixel segmentation.}
For each 2-slice unit $\mathcal{S}_j$, SLIC \cite{achanta2012slic} is applied to a
3-channel multi-modal stack (T1CE, T2, and FLAIR) to partition the brain tissue into
superpixel regions. Multi-modal clustering captures boundaries invisible on any single
modality: T1CE highlights gadolinium enhancement in active tumour regions, T2 reveals
oedema and fluid, and FLAIR delineates the full tumour extent with CSF suppression. By
fusing all three modalities, the algorithm produces superpixel boundaries that adhere
closely to complex tumour margins and oedema-tissue transitions, yielding more
clinically meaningful regions than clustering on T1CE alone.

The SLIC algorithm operates as follows. First, $K$ cluster centres are initialised
on a regular grid with spacing $S = \sqrt{N/K}$, where $N$ is the total number of
pixels. Each cluster centre is then shifted to the lowest-gradient position within a
$3 \times 3$ neighbourhood to avoid placing centres on edges. In each iteration,
every pixel is assigned to the nearest cluster centre within a $2S \times 2S$ search
window, using a composite distance metric that balances intensity similarity against
spatial proximity:
\[
    D_s(p,\,c_k) = \sqrt{d_I^2 + \left(\frac{d_{xy}}{S}\right)^2 m^2}
\]
where $d_I = |I_p - I_{c_k}|$ is the intensity distance between pixel $p$ and
cluster centre $c_k$, $d_{xy}$ is the Euclidean spatial distance, and $m$ is the
compactness parameter. After pixel assignment, cluster centres are updated to the
mean position and intensity of their assigned pixels, and the process repeats until
convergence.

The compactness parameter $m$ controls the trade-off between two competing
objectives. A high value of $m$ penalises spatial distance strongly, producing
superpixels with regular, approximately square shapes that cover the image uniformly
but may cross tissue boundaries. A low value of $m$ allows superpixels to deform
freely along intensity edges, producing irregularly shaped regions with strict boundary
adherence. We set $m = 0.1$ to favour strict boundary adherence over spatial regularity,
ensuring that superpixel boundaries follow the tumour margin and oedema-tissue
transitions as closely as possible, preserving critical clinical landmarks. A Gaussian
smoothing with $\sigma = 0.3$ is applied before clustering to reduce noise-induced
fragmentation without blurring significant tissue boundaries.

Parameters used: \texttt{n\_segments}=200, \texttt{compactness}=0.1,
\texttt{sigma}=0.3. The target of 200 segments per 2-slice unit results in
approximately 100 target superpixels per slice; however, SLIC eliminates
very small or empty clusters during post-processing, and the brain mask occupies
only a fraction of the $240 \times 240$ slice area, so the mean realised segment
count is $\approx$46 per slice. This level of spatial granularity produces
superpixels that are small enough to preserve tumour boundary detail while large
enough to aggregate sufficient voxels for stable intensity statistics within each
node feature vector.

\textbf{Step 4 --- Node feature extraction.}
Once SLIC has partitioned each 2-slice unit into superpixels, each superpixel must
be described by a fixed-length feature vector that encodes the information the GNN
needs to classify it as tumour or non-tumour. The feature vector is designed to
capture three complementary aspects of each superpixel: its multi-modal intensity
profile (how it looks across all four MRI sequences), its spatial position within
the brain, and its geometric shape. Together, these features provide a compact but
informative tissue signature.

Each superpixel $s_i$ is represented by a 15-dimensional feature vector
$\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{15}$:
\[
    \mathbf{x}_i =
    \bigl[\,
        \underbrace{\mu_{i,m},\; \sigma_{i,m}}_{\text{intensity stats},\; m \in \{
        \text{T1},\text{T1CE},\text{T2},\text{FLAIR}\}},\;
        \underbrace{|s_i|,\; \tilde{a}_i,\; \tilde{y}_i,\; \tilde{x}_i}_{\text{spatial}},\;
        \underbrace{P_i,\; C_i,\; R_i}_{\text{morphological}}
    \,\bigr]^\top
\]

\textbf{Intensity features (8 dimensions).} For each of the four MRI modalities
$m \in \{\text{T1}, \text{T1CE}, \text{T2}, \text{FLAIR}\}$, the mean intensity
$\mu_{i,m}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{i,m}$ are computed over all voxels
within superpixel $s_i$. The mean captures the average tissue signal in that
modality, while the standard deviation captures tissue heterogeneity within the
superpixel. Tumour regions tend to have higher T1CE mean intensity (due to
gadolinium enhancement), higher T2 and FLAIR mean intensity (due to oedema), and
higher intra-superpixel variance (due to mixed tissue types at tumour boundaries).
By including both mean and standard deviation from all four modalities, the feature
vector encodes a multi-contrast tissue fingerprint that is robust to scanner
variations (because the input volumes are Z-score normalised).

\textbf{Spatial features (4 dimensions).} The absolute superpixel area $|s_i|$
(pixel count), the area normalised by total slice area $\tilde{a}_i = |s_i| /
(240 \times 240)$, and the centroid coordinates $\tilde{y}_i$ and $\tilde{x}_i$
normalised to the range $[0,1]$ encode the superpixel's position and size within
the brain. Spatial features are informative because brain tumours have anatomical
location preferences: gliomas most commonly arise in the cerebral hemispheres, and
their spatial distribution is non-uniform. Normalising coordinates to $[0,1]$
ensures that the spatial features are scale-invariant and comparable across patients.

\textbf{Morphological features (3 dimensions).} The perimeter $P_i$ (boundary
pixel count), compactness $C_i = 4\pi|s_i|/P_i^2$ (a shape circularity measure
ranging from 0 for elongated shapes to 1 for perfect circles), and intensity range
$R_i = \max_{m}(\mu_{i,m}) - \min_m(\mu_{i,m})$ (the spread of mean intensities
across the four modalities) capture geometric and multi-modal contrast properties.
Tumour-boundary superpixels tend to have irregular shapes (lower compactness) and
higher intensity range (because the four modalities respond differently to abnormal
tissue), providing additional discriminative signal beyond the raw intensity
statistics.

No ground-truth label is included in $\mathbf{x}_i$. This exclusion is critical:
if the tumour label were included as a feature, the model would learn to use it
directly during training, resulting in perfect training accuracy but complete
failure at inference time when labels are unavailable. The absence of label
information in the features was verified programmatically before each training
fold to prevent data leakage. Specifically, the verification asserts that the
node feature matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 15}$ contains exactly
the 15 features defined above (8 intensity, 4 spatial, 3 morphological) and that
no column correlates with the label vector $\mathbf{y}$ above $r = 0.99$, which
would indicate direct label inclusion. This check runs automatically at graph
loading time before any training loop begins.

\subsection{Mathematical Description of Segmentation}
\label{sec:mathseg}

\textbf{Node label assignment.}
The binary node label $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ is assigned based on a fractional
tumour-volume threshold applied to voxel-level overlap between superpixel $s_i$ and the
Whole Tumour ground-truth mask $M_{\text{GT}}$:
\[
    y_i = \mathbf{1}\!\left[
        \frac{|s_i \cap M_{\text{GT}}|}{|s_i|} > 0.1
    \right]
\]
A fractional threshold of $>10\%$ is applied: a node is positive if more than one-tenth
of its constituent voxels lie within the Whole Tumour annotation. This threshold
preserves critical boundary information by capturing superpixels that have only partial
tumour content, enabling the model to learn tissue transition patterns within
boundary-adjacent regions. A lower threshold (10\% vs.~50\% majority vote) maximises
boundary sensitivity at the cost of introducing some label noise from regions that are
predominantly healthy but contain small tumour patches. This trade-off favours recall
over precision in the training objective, allowing the model to learn from gradual
tissue transitions. The ensemble classifier and inference threshold ($\tau_d = 0.5$)
provide a countervailing stabilisation mechanism that suppresses false positives
generated by label noise during inference. The choice of $>10\%$ follows best
practices in graph-based segmentation where fractional labelling preserves boundary
information critical for accurate margin localisation.

\textbf{GraphSAGE message passing.}
The core computation in the GraphSAGE network is the message-passing operation,
which iteratively updates each node's representation by combining its own features
with aggregated information from its neighbours. Intuitively, this allows each
superpixel to ``learn about'' the tissue properties of its surrounding region: after
one layer of message passing, each node knows about its immediate neighbours; after
two layers, it knows about neighbours-of-neighbours; and after $L$ layers, each
node has aggregated information from its entire $L$-hop neighbourhood. With 5 layers,
each superpixel node has a receptive field spanning five hops in the graph, which
covers a spatial extent large enough to encompass small to medium-sized tumour
regions in superpixel space.

The GraphSAGE operator \cite{hamilton2017graphsage} at layer $l$ computes node
embedding $\mathbf{h}_v^{(l)}$ by aggregating mean-pooled neighbourhood
representations concatenated with the node's own embedding:
\[
    \mathbf{h}_v^{(l)} = \sigma\!\left(
        \mathbf{W}^{(l)} \cdot \mathrm{CONCAT}\!\left(
            \mathbf{h}_v^{(l-1)},\;
            \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(v)|}
            \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \mathbf{h}_u^{(l-1)}
        \right)
    \right)
\]
where $\mathbf{W}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l \times 2d_{l-1}}$ is the learnable
weight matrix at layer $l$, $\mathcal{N}(v)$ is the one-hop neighbour set of node
$v$, and $\sigma$ is the ReLU non-linearity. We omit the $\ell_2$ normalisation
step present in the original GraphSAGE formulation \cite{hamilton2017graphsage};
Batch Normalisation, applied after each layer (described below), subsumes the role
of explicit embedding normalisation by standardising the activation distribution
across the batch dimension.

The operation proceeds in three steps at each layer. First, the mean-pooling
aggregation computes the element-wise average of all neighbour embeddings, producing
a single vector that summarises the local neighbourhood. Second, this aggregated
vector is concatenated with the node's own embedding from the previous layer,
creating a combined representation that preserves both the node's individual
identity and its neighbourhood context. Third, the concatenated vector is
transformed by a learnable weight matrix and passed through a non-linearity,
producing the updated node embedding for the next layer.

Each layer applies Batch Normalisation (BatchNorm) after the linear transformation
to stabilise training dynamics by normalising the distribution of activations across
the batch. This is followed by the ReLU activation function, which introduces
non-linearity, and Dropout at rate 0.1, which randomly zeroes 10\% of the
activations during training to prevent co-adaptation of features and improve
generalisation. The input dimension is $d_0 = 15$ (the 15-dimensional node feature
vector); layers 1--4 project to $d_l = 256$ hidden channels, providing sufficient
capacity to learn complex tissue patterns; and the final GraphSAGE layer reduces to
$d_5 = 64$, creating a compressed representation that is passed to the classification
head.

\textbf{Binary classification and loss.}
After the 5-layer GraphSAGE backbone produces a 64-dimensional embedding for each
node, a classification head must map this embedding to a binary decision: tumour or
non-tumour. A two-layer MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron) classifier performs this
mapping: Linear($64 \to 32$) reduces the dimensionality, ReLU introduces
non-linearity, Dropout(0.1) provides regularisation, and a final
Linear($32 \to 1$) produces a single scalar logit $z_v$ for each node. The logit
represents the model's unnormalised confidence that the node belongs to the tumour
class; positive values indicate tumour, negative values indicate background.

Training employs BCEWithLogitsLoss (Binary Cross-Entropy with Logits Loss), which
combines a sigmoid transformation with binary cross-entropy in a single numerically
stable operation, paired with a complementary Soft Dice component to optimise
overlap-based metrics. The primary weighted BCE loss compensates for severe class
imbalance:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \left[
        w^+ \cdot y_v \log \sigma(z_v) + (1 - y_v) \log(1 - \sigma(z_v))
    \right]
\]
where $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$ is the sigmoid function that maps the logit
to a probability in $[0, 1]$. The positive class weight $w^+ = 9.0$ is applied to
tumour nodes, scaling their loss contribution by a factor of 9 relative to background
nodes. The Soft Dice component directly optimises the Dice coefficient, the primary
evaluation metric, providing an auxiliary gradient signal that amplifies overlap
between predicted and ground-truth tumour regions. During development, the hybrid
loss (weighted BCE + Soft Dice) and pure weighted BCE were both evaluated; pure BCE
with $w^+ = 9.0$ produced superior generalisation on the held-out set, likely because
the Dice term introduces unstable gradient noise for small graphs ($\approx$92 nodes,
few tumour nodes per graph). The class weighting is necessary because the dataset
exhibits severe class imbalance at the superpixel level: approximately 95\% of
superpixels are non-tumour (background), and only 5\% contain tumour tissue, yielding
an approximate 19:1 background-to-tumour ratio. Without class weighting, the model
would achieve high accuracy by predicting all nodes as background, resulting in zero
sensitivity to tumour regions. The weight $w^+ = 9.0$ was selected empirically rather
than set to the reciprocal of the imbalance ratio (which would be $\approx 19$); the
reduced weight provides improved generalisation by accepting some false positives to
maintain adequate tumour sensitivity.

At inference, the decision threshold is $\tau_d = 0.5$:
$\hat{y}_v = \mathbf{1}[\sigma(z_v) \geq 0.5]$. This threshold can be adjusted
post-training for different clinical applications: lowering it increases sensitivity
(more tumour nodes detected) at the cost of precision (more false positives), which
may be appropriate for screening applications where missing a tumour is more
dangerous than flagging healthy tissue for review.

\textbf{Dice coefficient.}
The evaluation metric is the Sørensen-Dice coefficient computed at patient level
by aggregating node predictions to voxel masks:
\[
    \mathrm{Dice}(M_{\text{pred}},\, M_{\text{GT}}) =
    \frac{2\,|M_{\text{pred}} \cap M_{\text{GT}}| + \epsilon}
         {|M_{\text{pred}}| + |M_{\text{GT}}| + \epsilon}
\]
where $\epsilon = 10^{-7}$ is a smoothing constant that ensures numerical stability
when both masks are empty. In the edge case where the model predicts no tumour and
the ground truth also contains no tumour ($|M_{\text{pred}}| = |M_{\text{GT}}| = 0$),
the smoothed formula yields Dice~$\approx$~1.0, reflecting correct agreement on an
empty region. When only the prediction is empty but the ground truth contains tumour,
the formula yields Dice~$\approx$~0.0, correctly penalising a complete miss.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} summarises the complete inference procedure for a
single patient volume.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Superpixel-GNN Inference Pipeline}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\KwIn{MRI volume $V \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 240 \times 240 \times 155}$,
      brain mask $\mathcal{B}$,
      trained ensemble $\{\Phi_k\}_{k=1}^{5}$}
\KwOut{Binary whole-tumour mask $\hat{M} \in \{0,1\}^{240 \times 240 \times 155}$}
\BlankLine
\tcp{Stage 1: Preprocessing}
\For{each modality $m \in \{\mathrm{T1}, \mathrm{T1CE}, \mathrm{T2}, \mathrm{FLAIR}\}$}{
    Compute $\mu_m$, $\sigma_m$ over $\mathcal{B}$\;
    $\tilde{V}_m \leftarrow (V_m - \mu_m) / \sigma_m$ on $\mathcal{B}$\;
}
$\mathcal{A} \leftarrow$ active slices with brain-fraction $\geq 0.05$\;
$\{\mathcal{S}_j\} \leftarrow$ partition $\mathcal{A}$ into consecutive 2-slice units\;
\BlankLine
\tcp{Stage 2: Graph construction}
\ForEach{2-slice unit $\mathcal{S}_j$}{
    $\{s_i\} \leftarrow \mathrm{SLIC}(\tilde{V}_{\mathrm{T1CE}}[\mathcal{S}_j],\;
    K{=}200,\; m{=}0.1)$\;
    \ForEach{superpixel $s_i$}{
        $\mathbf{x}_i \leftarrow$ extract 15-dimensional feature vector\;
    }
    $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{intra}} \leftarrow$ RAG edges from SLIC adjacency\;
    $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{inter}} \leftarrow$ $k$NN edges across slices ($k{=}3$,
    IoU $> 0.1$, dist $< 10\,\mathrm{mm}$)\;
    $\mathcal{G}_j \leftarrow (\{s_i\},\; \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{intra}} \cup
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{inter}},\; \{\mathbf{x}_i\})$\;
}
\BlankLine
\tcp{Stage 3: Ensemble node classification}
\ForEach{graph $\mathcal{G}_j$}{
    \For{$k = 1$ \KwTo $5$}{
        $\mathbf{z}^{(k)} \leftarrow \Phi_k(\mathcal{G}_j)$ \tcp*{scalar logits per node}
    }

    }
}
\BlankLine
\tcp{Stage 4: Voxel mask reconstruction}
$\hat{M} \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$\;
\ForEach{superpixel $s_i$ with $\hat{y}_{s_i} = 1$}{

\subsection{Graph Construction}
\label{sec:graphconst}

\textbf{Formal graph definition.}
Each 2-slice MRI unit $\mathcal{S}_j$ is represented as an undirected attributed
graph $\mathcal{G}_j = (\mathcal{V}_j,\, \mathcal{E}_j,\, \mathbf{X}_j)$, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{V}_j = \{v_1, \dots, v_{n_j}\}$ is the node set; each node
    corresponds to one SLIC superpixel, $n_j \approx 92$ (2 slices
    $\times$ $\approx$46 superpixels per slice on average);
    \item $\mathcal{E}_j \subseteq \mathcal{V}_j \times \mathcal{V}_j$ is the
    edge set, partitioned as
    $\mathcal{E}_j = \mathcal{E}_j^{\text{intra}} \cup \mathcal{E}_j^{\text{inter}}$
    (intra-slice adjacency and inter-slice proximity edges);
    \item $\mathbf{X}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{n_j \times 15}$ is the node feature matrix,
    with row $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{15}$ as defined in
    Section~\ref{sec:mathpipeline}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Intra-slice edge set.}
Within each slice, edges connect superpixels that are physically adjacent ---
that is, superpixels whose pixel regions share a boundary in the image. This
connectivity encodes the spatial layout of tissue regions within the slice,
allowing the GNN to propagate information between neighbouring tissue
types. For example, a superpixel containing enhancing tumour can pass its
feature information to adjacent superpixels containing peritumoral oedema
or healthy tissue, enabling the model to learn that the presence of bright
T1CE signal in a neighbour increases the probability that the current node
is also part of the tumour region.

Formally, two nodes $v_i$ and $v_j$ within the same slice $z$ are connected by
an intra-slice edge if their corresponding superpixels are spatially adjacent in
the SLIC segmentation map, i.e., they share at least one pixel boundary:
\[
    (v_i, v_j) \in \mathcal{E}^{\text{intra}} \iff
    \exists\, p \in s_i,\, q \in s_j :
    \|p - q\|_1 = 1, \quad z(p) = z(q)
\]
This is equivalent to extracting the Region Adjacency Graph (RAG) from the SLIC
label map using 4-connectivity. In a RAG, each superpixel becomes a node, and an
edge is drawn between any two superpixels that share at least one boundary pixel.
The 4-connectivity criterion (checking only horizontal and vertical neighbours,
not diagonals) is sufficient because SLIC produces spatially contiguous regions
without gaps. For a slice with $K \approx 46$ superpixels, the resulting RAG has
$O(K)$ edges, typically yielding a sparse graph with a mean node degree of
approximately 3 to 5.

\textbf{Inter-slice edge set.}
Brain tumours are three-dimensional structures that extend across multiple axial
slices. If superpixels in adjacent slices are not connected, the GNN treats each
slice as an isolated graph with no information flow across the axial dimension.
This would force the model to classify each slice independently, ignoring the
volumetric continuity of the tumour. Inter-slice edges address this by connecting
superpixels in adjacent slices that correspond to the same anatomical region,
enabling the GNN's message-passing mechanism to propagate tumour-related
information along the axial axis.

However, inter-slice edges must be constructed carefully. Because SLIC is applied
independently to each slice, the superpixel partitions in adjacent slices are not
aligned --- the same anatomical region may be covered by differently shaped and
positioned superpixels in slice $z$ versus slice $z+1$. Naively connecting all
superpixels across slices would create a dense, uninformative edge set. We
therefore require three simultaneous criteria to establish an inter-slice edge:

\[
    (v_i, v_j) \in \mathcal{E}^{\text{inter}} \iff
    \mathrm{IoU}(s_i, s_j) > 0.1 \;\wedge\;
    \|\mathbf{c}_i - \mathbf{c}_j\|_2 < 10\,\text{mm} \;\wedge\;
    j \in \mathrm{kNN}_k(i),\; k = 3
\]

The first criterion, $\mathrm{IoU}(s_i, s_j) > 0.1$, requires that the two
superpixels have at least 10\% spatial overlap when projected onto the same 2D
plane. $\mathrm{IoU}(s_i, s_j) = |s_i \cap s_j| / |s_i \cup s_j|$ is the
Intersection-over-Union of the projected pixel sets. This ensures that connected
superpixels actually cover overlapping brain regions rather than being spatially
disjoint. The second criterion, $\|\mathbf{c}_i - \mathbf{c}_j\|_2 < 10\,\text{mm}$,
limits the Euclidean distance between superpixel centroids to 10 millimetres,
preventing long-range connections between distant brain regions that happen to have
similar features but no anatomical relationship. The third criterion,
$j \in \mathrm{kNN}_k(i)$ with $k = 3$, restricts each superpixel to at most
three cross-slice connections (the three nearest neighbours by centroid distance),
preventing any single superpixel from having an excessive number of inter-slice
edges that would dominate the message-passing computation.

These three conditions jointly ensure inter-slice connections are spatially
coherent and topologically meaningful: connected superpixels overlap
geometrically, are close in physical space, and are among each other's nearest
cross-slice neighbours.

\textbf{Example.} For a representative patient with 137 active slices, the graph
construction produces $\approx$68 graph units. Each unit contains on average
$n_j \approx 92$ nodes and $|\mathcal{E}_j| \approx 180$ edges (130 intra-slice
+ 50 inter-slice), yielding a mean node degree of $\approx$3.9. The full patient
graph representation contains $\approx$3,909 nodes compared to 8,928,000 raw
voxels --- a compression ratio of $\approx$2,284$\times$.

\textbf{Spatial compression.} The measured compression ratio from full voxel-space
to graph-node-space is:
\begin{equation}
    R_{\text{measured}} = \frac{\text{total brain voxels per patient}}
    {\text{mean graph nodes per patient}}
    = \frac{8{,}928{,}000}{3{,}909} \approx 2{,}284\times
    \label{eq:compression}
\end{equation}
where $8{,}928{,}000 = 240 \times 240 \times 155$ total voxels and 3,909 is the
empirically measured mean node count per patient across the 1,000-patient
cross-validation cohort. This ratio reflects the combined effect of background
removal (only brain-fraction voxels are active), slice-level SLIC compression, and
the 2-slice graph grouping strategy.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:training}

The training protocol was designed to maximise model performance within the
hardware constraints of a single NVIDIA RTX 2060 GPU with 6\,GB VRAM, which
imposes strict limits on batch size, model size, and the use of mixed precision.

\textbf{Loss function.}
Training uses BCEWithLogitsLoss with positive class weight $w^+ = 9.0$, as
described in Section~\ref{sec:mathseg}. An alternative hybrid loss combining
BCE with Dice loss was tested during preliminary experiments. The Dice loss
component directly optimises the Dice coefficient, which is the primary
evaluation metric. However, the hybrid loss produced lower ensemble Dice on
the held-out set compared to pure weighted BCE. This is likely because the
Dice loss operates at the graph level (computing overlap across all nodes in a
graph), and for small graphs with only $\approx$92 nodes and a few tumour
nodes, the gradient signal from Dice loss becomes noisy and unstable. Pure BCE
with class weighting provides a per-node gradient signal that is more stable
for small graphs and gave the best generalisation.

\textbf{Optimiser.}
AdamW \cite{loshchilov2019adamw} is used with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$
and weight decay of 0.01. AdamW decouples weight decay from the adaptive
learning rate, applying L2 regularisation directly to the parameters rather
than through the gradient. This decoupling improves generalisation compared
to the original Adam optimiser, particularly when using learning rate
schedules. The learning rate of $10^{-3}$ is the standard starting point for
Adam-family optimisers and was confirmed through preliminary experiments to
provide stable convergence.

\textbf{Learning rate schedule.}
A OneCycleLR scheduler implements a one-cycle cosine annealing policy. The
learning rate warms up from $\sim$$10^{-5}$ to the maximum of $10^{-3}$ over
the first 30\% of total training steps (the \texttt{pct\_start} parameter),
then decays following a cosine curve to a very small final value. The warm-up
phase prevents the large initial gradients that can destabilise training when
the model weights are randomly initialised. The subsequent cosine decay
progressively reduces the learning rate, allowing the model to converge to a
sharper minimum in the loss landscape. This schedule has been shown to achieve
faster convergence and better generalisation than fixed learning rates or
step-decay schedules, and it eliminates the need to manually tune a decay
schedule.

\textbf{Batch size and gradient accumulation.}
The per-step batch size of 24 graphs is the largest that fits within 6\,GB
VRAM when using Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) with FP16 computation. AMP
performs forward passes in half-precision (16-bit floating point), reducing
memory usage and enabling larger batch sizes, while maintaining a master copy
of the weights in full precision (32-bit) for numerically stable gradient
updates. Gradient accumulation over 2 steps doubles the effective batch size
to 48 graphs per parameter update. A larger effective batch size provides more
stable gradient estimates, reducing training noise and improving convergence.

\textbf{Early stopping and epoch budget.}
An early stopping criterion monitors the validation Dice with a patience of
10 epochs: if the validation Dice does not improve for 10 consecutive epochs,
training terminates to prevent overfitting. The maximum epoch budget is 50.
In practice, all five folds ran the full 50 epochs without early stopping
triggering, indicating that the model continued to improve (or at least did
not degrade) throughout the full training period. Complete hyperparameter
values are listed in Table~\ref{tab:training} (Section~\ref{sec:expsetup}).

\subsection{Ensemble Inference}
\label{sec:ensemble}

Ensemble methods combine the predictions of multiple independently trained models
to produce a single, more robust prediction. The motivation is that individual
models, even when trained with the same architecture and hyperparameters, learn
slightly different decision boundaries due to differences in their training data
(different fold partitions) and stochastic training dynamics (random weight
initialisation, dropout masks, data loading order). By averaging their predictions,
the ensemble reduces the variance of the final output, suppressing individual
model errors that are unlikely to be correlated across all five models.

The 5-fold cross-validation procedure (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}) produces five
independently trained GraphSAGE models $\Phi_1, \dots, \Phi_5$. For each fold $k$,
the checkpoint with the highest validation Dice across all 50 epochs is saved as
the fold-best model. No additional training, weight averaging, or distillation is
performed --- the five fold-best checkpoints are used directly at inference time.

Two complementary inference modes are defined: \textbf{single-fold inference} and
\textbf{5-fold soft-voting ensemble inference}. Both use identical model
architecture and hyperparameters; they differ in the number of checkpoints applied
and how probability estimates are aggregated.

\subsubsection{Single-Fold Inference}

In single-fold inference, a single fold-best model $\Phi_k$ is applied
independently to each graph $\mathcal{G}_j$. The model produces a per-node logit
$z_v^{(k)}$ through the 5-layer GraphSAGE backbone and 2-layer MLP classifier
described in Section~\ref{sec:mathseg}. The per-node prediction is:
\[
    P_k(v) = \sigma(z_v^{(k)}),
    \quad
    \hat{y}_k(v) = \mathbf{1}\!\left[P_k(v) \geq 0.5\right]
\]
where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. This mode serves as the baseline for
measuring the ensemble lift.

\subsubsection{5-Fold Soft-Voting Ensemble Inference}

In ensemble inference, all five fold-best checkpoints $\{\Phi_k\}_{k=1}^{5}$ are
applied to every graph $\mathcal{G}_j$. Each model produces an independent set of
per-node logits. The ensemble probability is the arithmetic mean of the five
sigmoid-transformed logits (soft voting):
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{ens}}(v) = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{5}
    \sigma\!\left(z_v^{(k)}\right),
    \quad
    \hat{y}_{\mathrm{ens}}(v) = \mathbf{1}\!\left[P_{\mathrm{ens}}(v) \geq 0.5\right]
    \label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}

The equal-weight averaging reduces prediction variance across folds without
introducing additional hyperparameters. Ensemble diversity arises naturally from
the different 720-patient training partitions in each fold; all five models share
the same architecture and hyperparameters.

\textbf{Voxel mask reconstruction.}
The patient-level prediction is obtained by aggregating all per-graph node
predictions across the patient's $\approx$68 graph units. Nodes classified as
tumour are back-projected to voxel space through the stored superpixel-to-voxel
mapping, producing the final binary mask
$\hat{M} \in \{0,1\}^{240 \times 240 \times 155}$. Quantitative results for
both inference modes are reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Efficiency Benchmarking}

Both the GNN and the 3D U-Net baseline were evaluated on identical hardware
(NVIDIA RTX 2060, 6\,GB VRAM) with the same patient splits and training budget
(50 epochs, binary BCEWithLogitsLoss, positive weight 9.0). The U-Net achieves
87.84\% Dice under these constraints. Published multi-class U-Net scores (91--92\%)
benefit from auxiliary gradient signal across ET/TC/WT sub-regions unavailable
in the binary formulation; they are not direct comparators.

An important distinction exists between inference and training memory
requirements. While the 3D U-Net fits within 6\,GB VRAM at inference
(2,500\,MB peak), \emph{training} is severely constrained: the 69.1M parameters
force an effective batch size of only 4 (batch 1 with gradient accumulation 4)
on $96^3$ patches, degrading optimisation dynamics and Instance Normalisation
statistics. The GNN, by contrast, trains comfortably at effective batch size 48
with 11\,MB peak memory. Published nnU-Net configurations require 32--40\,GB
VRAM for their auto-configured training pipeline (large patches, multi-class
cascades, full batch normalisation) and cannot train on 6\,GB hardware at all.

performance) and is clinically more consequential. Patient BraTS2021\_01594 achieved
perfect Dice (100\%) across 8,067 tumour superpixels, confirming that full
discrimination is achievable when T1CE contrast is strong.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Efficiency as a Structural Property}

The central result is that a 439K-parameter GNN trained on sparse superpixel graphs
matches or exceeds the accuracy of a 69.1M-parameter U-Net trained under identical
hardware constraints, while consuming 227$\times$ less GPU memory and running
5.9$\times$ faster end-to-end. This is not a training-trick result. The efficiency
advantage is \textit{structural}: it arises from the fundamental difference between
dense 3D convolution on 8.9M voxels and message-passing on $\approx$3,909 graph
nodes per patient. The parameter reduction, memory savings, and speedup hold
regardless of how well either model is individually tuned.

To understand why this structural difference exists, consider what each approach
computes. A 3D U-Net applies learned convolutional filters at every voxel position
in the $240 \times 240 \times 155$ volume. Each filter slides across the entire
volume, performing multiply-accumulate operations at every spatial location,
regardless of whether that location contains tumour, healthy brain, or empty
background. The computational cost is proportional to the total number of voxels
multiplied by the number of filters at each layer. In contrast, the GraphSAGE
network operates on $\approx$3,909 nodes per patient. At each layer, each node
aggregates features from its $\approx$3--5 neighbours and transforms the result
through a weight matrix. The total number of operations per layer is proportional
to the number of nodes multiplied by the number of edges --- both of which are
orders of magnitude smaller than the voxel grid. The 2,284$\times$ spatial
compression from voxels to graph nodes translates directly into proportional
reductions in memory and computation.

Three qualifications apply to this comparison. First, the 3D U-Net baseline is
trained with binary-only labels; published multi-class U-Net results (91--92\% WT
Dice) benefit from auxiliary gradient signal across ET/TC/WT sub-regions unavailable
in the binary formulation. In multi-class training, the network receives separate
supervision for Enhancing Tumour, Tumour Core, and Whole Tumour, which provides
richer gradient information that helps the encoder learn more discriminative features.
A fully-tuned binary U-Net on larger hardware would likely narrow the accuracy gap
to near-zero, but would not alter the structural efficiency advantages, because the
efficiency gains come from the data representation (graph vs.\ voxel grid), not from
the model being under-trained.

Second, graph construction adds $\approx$1.66\,s overhead per patient at first build
time, which is dominated by the CPU-based SLIC superpixel computation. This overhead
is a one-time cost per patient: once graphs are constructed, they can be cached to
disk and reused for multiple inference passes, reducing subsequent inference to the
75.4\,ms GNN-only figure. In clinical batch-processing workflows where graphs are
pre-built during an overnight preprocessing step, the inference-time cost is
effectively the 75.4\,ms figure rather than the 1.73\,s end-to-end figure.

Third, the 15 handcrafted features encode domain knowledge specific to glioma
imaging --- for instance, the importance of T1CE mean intensity as a marker of
enhancement, and the relevance of FLAIR signal for oedema detection. These features
would need to be redesigned for tumour types with different imaging characteristics,
such as meningiomas (which enhance uniformly on T1CE) or metastases (which may be
small and multifocal). An alternative approach would be to replace the handcrafted
features with learned features from a small CNN encoder, trading some interpretability
for broader applicability.

\subsection{The Complete-Miss Failure Mode}

5\% of per-graph slices receive near-zero Dice scores, concentrated among patients
with atypical or absent T1CE enhancement. This failure mode is qualitatively
different from boundary imprecision: the model predicts all-background, resulting
in the patient being missed entirely rather than partially segmented.

The root cause is the pipeline's reliance on T1CE as the primary SLIC clustering
channel and the dominant signal in the node feature vector. In standard glioblastoma
imaging, the enhancing tumour region appears as a bright ring or mass on T1CE,
creating strong intensity gradients that SLIC captures as well-defined superpixel
boundaries. The resulting node features show a clear contrast between tumour and
background superpixels. However, in low-grade gliomas and certain tumour subtypes,
gadolinium enhancement may be minimal or absent \cite{louis2016glioma}. In these
cases, the T1CE channel shows little contrast between tumour and healthy tissue,
and the SLIC superpixels do not align with tumour boundaries. The node features
for tumour superpixels become indistinguishable from background superpixels, and
the classifier assigns all nodes to the background class.

Three worst-case patients (BraTS2021\_01405, \_01366, \_01407) received near-zero
Dice scores ($\leq 5\times10^{-8}$). Visual inspection of their T1CE volumes
confirmed minimal or absent enhancement, consistent with this analysis.

\textbf{Any clinical deployment must include a post-processing gate that detects
all-zero output masks and flags them for mandatory radiologist review.} A simple
rule --- if the predicted mask contains zero tumour voxels or if the total predicted
tumour volume is below a clinically implausible threshold (e.g., $< 1$\,cm$^3$) ---
would catch all complete-miss cases and route them for manual review. This safety
gate does not solve the underlying limitation but prevents the most consequential
failure mode (missing a tumour entirely) from reaching clinical decisions.

Addressing the root cause requires one or more of the following: (a) adaptive
superpixel granularity that uses finer regions near low-contrast boundaries where
standard SLIC produces overly coarse partitions; (b) augmentation of atypical
enhancement cases during training, so the model learns to rely on T2 and FLAIR
signals when T1CE contrast is weak; or (c) learned feature representations from a
small convolutional encoder that can capture subtle texture patterns invisible to
handcrafted intensity statistics.

\subsection{Comparison with State-of-the-Art}

Table~\ref{tab:sota} contextualises our results against published methods on the
BraTS 2021 benchmark. The comparison requires careful interpretation because the
methods differ in task formulation, model capacity, hardware, and training data
composition.

Our binary ensemble (91.41\%) is within 1.3--1.9\,pp of leading multi-class methods
that solve the harder three-class segmentation problem (Enhancing Tumour, Tumour
Core, and Whole Tumour simultaneously). Although the reported WT Dice metric is
itself a binary measure (all tumour vs.\ background), the models that produce it
are trained with simultaneous ET, TC, and WT supervision. These three loss signals
share a common encoder, providing richer gradient information than the single
binary signal available to our method. The multi-class encoder learns to
distinguish enhancing tissue from necrosis and oedema from normal white matter
--- discriminative features that propagate through shared weights and improve WT
prediction even though WT collapses these sub-regions into a single class. This
training-regime advantage is well established: Isensee et al.\
\cite{isensee2021nnunet} report that multi-class training consistently improves
per-region Dice over independent binary models, even when only one region is
evaluated. The 1.3--1.9\,pp gap between our binary ensemble and leading
multi-class methods therefore reflects a systematic training disadvantage, not a
fundamental limitation of graph-based segmentation.

\textbf{Why the constrained U-Net underperforms its published potential.}
The 87.84\% Dice achieved by our 69.1M-parameter 3D U-Net baseline warrants
explanation, given that published multi-class 3D U-Net configurations achieve
$\sim$91\% WT Dice. Two factors account for the difference. First, binary-only
training removes the auxiliary gradient signal from Enhancing Tumour and Tumour
Core sub-region boundaries. In multi-class training, the shared encoder receives
supervision from three distinct boundary types, each contributing gradient
information that helps the encoder learn features discriminative at multiple
spatial scales. Under binary training, only the outer whole-tumour boundary
provides supervision, and the encoder must learn all discriminative features from
this single signal. Second, the 6\,GB VRAM constraint restricts the U-Net to an
effective batch size of 4 (batch 1, gradient accumulation 4) on $96^3$ patches.
Small-batch training degrades Batch Normalisation statistics and increases
gradient noise, both of which disproportionately affect large models with deep
encoder--decoder pathways. By contrast, the GNN trains at effective batch size 48
on compact superpixel graphs, and its 15-dimensional handcrafted features already
encode the clinical tissue contrasts (T1CE enhancement, FLAIR oedema signal) that
the U-Net encoder must learn from raw voxels under constrained conditions.

These are not artificial handicaps. They reflect the hardware and task constraints
that define deployment in resource-limited clinical settings --- the scenario this
work targets. A fully-tuned multi-class U-Net on high-end hardware would narrow
the accuracy gap, but would not alter the 157$\times$ parameter reduction,
227$\times$ memory savings, or 5.9$\times$ inference speedup, because these
advantages are structural properties of the graph representation, not artefacts
of the baseline being under-trained.

Compared with prior GNN-based brain tumour segmentation, our results represent a
substantial advance. Saueressig et al.\ \cite{saueressig2021exploring} reported
Dice scores between 82\% and 86\% on BraTS 2019, but on a smaller patient subset
and without ensemble evaluation or controlled efficiency benchmarking. Patel et al.\
\cite{patel2023multiclass} achieved 84.3\% Dice with 10.2M parameters on BraTS
2021, but used a GAT architecture on a Tesla K80 without a CNN baseline comparison.
Our ensemble Dice of 91.41\% with 439K parameters per model represents a
$+$5--9\,pp accuracy improvement over these prior GNN results, while using
2--23$\times$ fewer parameters.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Generalisation Analysis}

The zero-shot evaluation on BraTS 2023 (89.40\% Dice, 2.01\,pp gap from BraTS
2021 held-out performance) provides evidence that the superpixel-graph
representation captures features that generalise across dataset editions. This
is notable because BraTS 2021 and BraTS 2023 differ in patient composition,
contributing institutions, and potentially in scanner hardware and acquisition
protocols \cite{menze2015multimodal, bakas2021brats}. Standard volumetric CNN
pipelines typically retrain for each new dataset edition
\cite{isensee2021nnunet}; that our model achieves 89.40\% Dice without any
retraining or threshold adjustment suggests that the handcrafted features and
graph-based learning capture scanner-invariant tissue properties.

The observation that sensitivity \textit{improves} on BraTS 2023
(87.77\%~$\rightarrow$~90.69\%) while precision drops
(95.52\%~$\rightarrow$~92.46\%) indicates a specific form of distribution
shift. The model detects more tumour regions in BraTS 2023 (higher
sensitivity), but some of these detections are false positives (lower
precision). This pattern is consistent with scanner diversity: different
scanner manufacturers produce slightly different intensity profiles, and some
normal tissue regions in BraTS 2023 may have intensity patterns that resemble
tumour tissue in BraTS 2021. The elevated per-patient Dice standard deviation
($\pm$11.10\% on BraTS 2023 vs.\ $\pm$6.08\% on BraTS 2021) reflects the
broader morphological and scanner diversity of the larger 2023 cohort.

\subsection{Deployment Implications}

The 75.4\,ms (pre-built graph) / 1.73\,s (end-to-end) inference times, 11\,MB peak
GPU memory, and 1.7\,MB model weight file collectively enable deployment scenarios
that are structurally infeasible for volumetric methods:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consumer-grade hardware.} Accurate inference on a mid-range
    desktop GPU removes the hardware barrier for clinics in low- and middle-income
    countries that cannot procure data-centre-class accelerators. The RTX 2060 used
    in this work is a consumer gaming card that costs a fraction of the data-centre
    GPUs (A100, V100) typically required for volumetric segmentation models. The
    11\,MB peak GPU memory means the model can also run on older or lower-end GPUs
    with as little as 1\,GB VRAM, further expanding accessibility.
    \item \textbf{Edge and embedded deployment.} A 1.7\,MB model can be loaded onto
    embedded inference hardware such as the NVIDIA Jetson series, enabling
    point-of-care deployment in mobile imaging units or telemedicine devices where
    network connectivity to cloud computing resources may be unreliable.
    \item \textbf{High-throughput screening.} 11\,MB peak memory allows multiple
    concurrent inference processes on the same 6\,GB VRAM device, enabling batch
    screening workflows where dozens of patients can be processed simultaneously.
    For a hospital processing 40 cases per day, the 1.73\,s end-to-end time per
    patient means all cases could be processed in under two minutes total, compared
    to over six minutes with the 3D U-Net baseline.
    \item \textbf{Interactive clinical use.} 75.4\,ms per-patient inference with
    pre-built graphs enables real-time feedback within a radiologist's review session.
    A radiologist scrolling through cases could receive instant segmentation overlays,
    supporting a review workflow where AI predictions are available before the
    radiologist begins manual assessment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We presented a superpixel-graph formulation for binary brain tumour segmentation
that achieves \textbf{91.41\% ensemble Dice} on a sealed 251-patient BraTS 2021
held-out set with \textbf{439,041 parameters}, \textbf{75.4\,ms} per-patient GNN
inference, and \textbf{11\,MB} peak GPU memory. Five-fold cross-validation confirms
\textbf{90.02\%\,$\pm$\,0.66\%} Dice with a 1.76\,pp inter-fold range. Zero-shot
transfer to BraTS 2023 yields \textbf{89.40\%} Dice (2.01\,pp gap, 1,245 patients),
demonstrating cross-edition robustness without any retraining. End-to-end processing
per patient requires \textbf{1,732\,ms} including on-the-fly SLIC graph construction,
a \textbf{5.9$\times$ speedup} over a hardware-matched 3D U-Net; for pre-cached
graphs, GNN inference takes \textbf{75.4\,ms}.

Systematic ablation on Fold 0 confirms the 5-layer, 256-dim GraphSAGE configuration
as the most parameter-efficient competitive configuration: added depth provides no
accuracy benefit, while the wider 512-dim variant achieves $+$4.75\,pp at
3.9$\times$ the parameter cost (1.71M --- still 40$\times$ smaller than the 3D
U-Net). The 512-dim variant was trained for only 30 epochs (vs.\ 50 for the
baseline), so its advantage may narrow under matched training; systematic
exploration of wider architectures is identified as a priority for future work. The
dominant failure mode---complete-miss predictions on patients with atypical T1CE
enhancement---is characterised, and a mandatory empty-prediction detection gate is
specified as a prerequisite for any clinical deployment.

These results demonstrate that graph-structured representations constitute a
practically deployable alternative to volumetric convolution for brain tumour
detection, enabling AI-assisted diagnostics on consumer-grade hardware where
3D CNN-based pipelines are not feasible.

\textbf{Limitations.}
Several limitations of this work must be acknowledged. First, this work addresses
binary whole-tumour segmentation only. The BraTS benchmark defines three clinically
relevant sub-regions --- Enhancing Tumour (ET), Tumour Core (TC), and Whole Tumour
(WT) --- and state-of-the-art methods segment all three simultaneously. Multi-class
methods benefit from auxiliary gradient signal across sub-regions: the supervision
from ET and TC boundaries helps the shared encoder learn more discriminative
features that indirectly improve WT segmentation. The 1.3--1.9\,pp gap between our
binary ensemble (91.41\%) and leading multi-class methods (nnU-Net at 92.7\%,
Swin-UNETR at 93.3\%) is therefore partially attributable to this formulation
difference rather than a fundamental limitation of the graph-based approach.

Second, cross-dataset evaluation is limited to BraTS 2021~$\rightarrow$~2023, both
of which contain adult gliomas acquired under similar protocols. Performance on
non-glioma tumour types (meningioma, metastases, paediatric brain tumours) and
post-treatment imaging (where surgical cavities and radiation necrosis alter the
tissue appearance) is untested. These scenarios present different imaging
characteristics that may require modified feature engineering.

Third, the 15 handcrafted node features encode domain knowledge specific to glioma
MRI imaging. Designing these features required understanding of which MRI modalities
carry which clinical information and how tumour tissue differs from healthy tissue
in each modality. Applying the pipeline to a new imaging domain would require
redesigning the feature vector, which limits the approach's generalisability
compared to end-to-end learned representations that automatically extract relevant
features from raw data.

Fourth, the SLIC-based graph construction is performed on CPU and constitutes the
dominant component of end-to-end inference time (1.66\,s out of 1.73\,s total).
While graph caching eliminates this cost for repeated inference, the CPU-based SLIC
computation limits real-time performance in scenarios where pre-caching is not
feasible.

\textbf{Future work.}
Several directions for extending this work are identified. First, multi-task
extension for simultaneous ET/TC/WT prediction would enable direct comparison
with state-of-the-art multi-class methods and potentially improve WT Dice through
the auxiliary gradient signal from sub-region supervision. This requires modifying
the node label assignment to produce three binary labels per node and adjusting the
classification head to output three logits instead of one.

Second, GPU-accelerated SLIC implementations could reduce the graph construction
time from 1.66\,s to under 100\,ms per patient, bringing end-to-end inference time
close to the 75.4\,ms GNN-only figure and enabling true real-time performance
without pre-caching.

Third, adaptive superpixel granularity could address the complete-miss failure mode
by generating finer superpixels in regions of low contrast, where standard SLIC
produces overly coarse partitions that fail to capture subtle tumour boundaries.

Fourth, the 512-dimensional GraphSAGE variant produced a $+$4.75\,pp Dice gain
over the 256-dim baseline in ablation, albeit under a reduced training budget
(30 vs.\ 50 epochs). At 1.71M parameters it remains 40$\times$ smaller than the
3D U-Net and fits comfortably within 6\,GB VRAM, making it a strong candidate for
future investigation under matched training conditions. Systematic exploration of
hidden dimension, combined with the multi-class extension above, could further
narrow the gap to published volumetric methods while preserving the efficiency
advantages of graph-based segmentation.

Fifth, prospective radiologist evaluation studies are essential before any clinical
deployment can be considered. Such studies would measure the impact of the model's
predictions on radiologist accuracy, confidence, and reading time in a controlled
clinical environment, and are a prerequisite for regulatory pathway consideration.
